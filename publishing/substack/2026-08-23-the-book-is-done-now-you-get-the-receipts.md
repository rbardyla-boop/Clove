# The Book Is Done. Now You Get the Receipts.

The book is uploaded.

Amazon gets to stare at it for a while and decide whether I have successfully obeyed the sacred geometry of margins, metadata and capitalism.

If everything clears review, **October 9** is release day.

Also my birthday.

Apparently I decided one stressful event per day was cowardice.

But finishing the book creates a new problem.

I spent months making claims about democracy, political persuasion, voter data, algorithms, behavioural science, social proof, political tribes, government power, Trump, Trudeau, the World Economic Forum, COVID messaging, privacy, and the weird machinery between an event happening and an opinion arriving in your head.

Then I kept running into the same rule:

> **A beautiful paragraph does not outrank an ugly receipt.**

So the research cannot end at the back cover.

That is what I am building at **CloveLearn.io** between now and release day.

Not a fan club for the book.

Not a page explaining why I was right.

A public evidence layer.

The research programme starts with an intentionally annoying rule: **do not prove the preferred story.**

Do not begin with “democracy is fake.”

Do not begin with “voters are controlled.”

Do not begin with “elites run everything.”

Do not begin with “the algorithm made everybody insane.”

Turn those into questions and make them survive contact with evidence.

That means keeping things that ruin a good argument.

Null results.

Small effects.

Legal changes that make an old criticism obsolete.

Evidence that users are not passive idiots.

Evidence that governments sometimes have legitimate reasons to influence behaviour.

Evidence that political parties need data to function.

Evidence that a conspiracy-shaped outcome can emerge from ordinary incentives without anybody sitting in a volcano headquarters stroking a white cat.

And when a claim dies, leave the body where people can see it.

CloveLearn is also getting another project that taught me the same lesson from the technical side: **AIS F1-REPLAY**.

That was a forensic attempt to replay old AI-system work instead of trusting my own notes and memory about what had worked.

Some things reproduced.

Some did not.

One first Powerplant run had **656 passing tests, 13 failures and 20 skips**. Proto hit **six collection errors**. A Poly replay never even got to execution because the isolated token could not access the archived private repository.

Those failures stay in the record.

Later narrower passes do not get to travel back in time and erase them.

That principle is now carrying over into the political research:

**Do the experiment. Keep the first result. State the boundary. Do not promote what you did not establish.**

The book ends with eight checks I kept using on myself:

1. Swap the jerseys.
2. Remove the name.
3. Show me the fucking denominator.
4. Who picked this for me?
5. Find the primary receipt.
6. Do the archive pass.
7. Ask what would change my mind.
8. Put the bastard in the chair.

That last one may be my favourite.

Whenever somebody proposes giving government, a platform, a party or an institution more power because the current people in charge are trustworthy, imagine the person you distrust most sitting behind the same desk tomorrow.

Same database.

Same emergency power.

Same surveillance authority.

Same moderation tool.

Same legal precedent.

Still like the design?

If not, maybe it was never a principle.

Maybe it was a temporary advantage.

So that is the job between now and October 9.

The book is done.

Now I build the place where you can check whether the bastard who wrote it did his homework.

And, for the love of God, **do not trust me either.**

Check the receipts.
