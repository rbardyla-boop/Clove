TITLE: WHAT DO I MEAN WHEN I SAY I MADE UP MY OWN MIND?
SUBTITLE: Nobody thinks in a vacuum. Agency is deciding what gets to influence you.
SCHEDULE: October 27, 2026

“I made up my own mind.”

Did you?

From what?

Raised by wolves?

Born in a cave with no language, no parents, no school, no television, no religion, no politics, no advertising, no friends and no uncle who ruined Christmas by explaining interest rates?

Of course not.

Every human mind is influenced.

That is not the scandal.

The question is what kind of influence occurred and whether you retained meaningful room to inspect it.

I think we have wrecked the word manipulation by using it for everything we dislike.

A newspaper headline frames an issue?

Manipulation.

A politician gives a speech?

Manipulation.

Your wife says the shirt makes you look like a divorced magician?

Possibly accurate information.

Humans constantly select, frame and persuade.

We cannot build a society where nobody influences anybody.

We would have to ban conversation.

So I need a better ladder.

Information:

Here are facts or claims you can consider.

Persuasion:

I am openly trying to change what you think or do by giving you reasons.

Framing:

I am choosing which aspects of the situation to emphasize.

Targeting:

I am choosing which audience receives which message.

Optimization:

I am measuring responses and changing the message to improve some outcome.

Deception:

I am intentionally giving you false or materially misleading information.

Manipulation:

This is the harder one.

For me, the concern rises when the persuader exploits something about the target in a way that reduces reflective choice — especially when the method or intent is hidden.

Coercion:

Now the choice is being constrained by threats, force or serious penalties.

These are not perfect legal definitions.

They are a practical map.

And the boundaries can overlap.

A government advertisement can be persuasion.

A misleading government advertisement can be deception.

A government saying “support this policy or we will imprison you” has moved into coercive territory.

An algorithm deciding which argument you see is framing and selection.

If the system is continuously learning which emotional message keeps you engaged, optimization enters the picture.

None of those words tells us automatically whether the outcome is good or bad.

A public-health campaign may use persuasion to save lives.

A parent may frame a dangerous choice in the clearest possible terms.

A teacher may target an explanation to the student who did not understand the first one.

Optimization itself is not evil.

I optimize the thermostat.

The moral problem depends on the relationship.

What is being optimized?

Who chose the goal?

What does the persuader know?

What does the target know?

Can the target inspect the claim?

Can the target refuse?

What happens if they do?

That last question matters.

A billboard can try to persuade me all day.

I can tell the billboard to fuck off and keep driving.

A boss, government, school, doctor, platform or intimate partner can have power that makes refusal more complicated.

So agency is not some magical state where no influence touched you.

Agency is the capacity to notice influences, evaluate them, resist some, accept others and remain responsible for the choice where genuine choice exists.

That means I can be influenced and still make up my own mind.

In fact, making up my mind requires influence.

I need evidence.

Arguments.

Other people correcting me.

History.

Experts.

Experience.

The goal is not an uncontaminated brain.

That would be an empty one.

The goal is a brain with a fucking customs desk.

What are you bringing in?

Where did it come from?

Why should I trust it?

What would make me send it back?

That is the kind of independence I care about.

Not:

Nobody told me what to think.

But:

Lots of people tried.

I inspected the cargo.

BORING RECEIPT

This essay offers a working conceptual framework, not settled legal or philosophical definitions. Academic debates about manipulation disagree over whether concealed intent, exploitation of vulnerability, deception, bypassing reflective agency or other conditions are necessary. The practical distinctions here are intended for Clove's agency work and should be refined when applied to law, medicine or specific political cases.

A useful philosophical starting point is the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on manipulation, which surveys competing accounts and their difficulties.

Source:
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-manipulation/

TITLE: THE PERSON GETS VETO POWER OVER THE STEREOTYPE
SUBTITLE: A group pattern can be a clue. It can never be the sentence.
SCHEDULE: October 30, 2026

Humans love categories.

Categories are efficient.

Dog.

Truck.

Doctor.

Teenager.

Electrician.

Conservative.

Woman.

Man.

The brain sees a category and starts loading expectations before the individual has even sat down.

Sometimes that is useful.

If somebody tells me a Labrador is coming through the door, I am more likely to expect a wagging tail than a PowerPoint on municipal zoning.

But people are where this gets dangerous.

We learn real group statistics.

Then somebody quietly turns a population pattern into a personal verdict.

Men are more likely to do X.

Therefore this man did X.

Women average Y.

Therefore this woman is Y.

People from this class vote Z.

Therefore I already know what this person believes.

No.

The group does not know the fucking person.

This is where I want an extremely simple rule:

The stereotype can speak first.

The person gets the final word.

Technically, I would put it like this:

Population pattern.

Prior expectation.

Context.

Individual evidence.

Update.

More individual evidence.

Update again.

Actual behaviour.

Verdict.

The mistake is stopping at step one because the statistic made us feel scientific.

Suppose a doctor knows men, on average, may present or seek help differently from women in some setting.

Useful clue.

Then the man sitting in front of her tells her exactly how he feels.

His direct evidence should begin overruling the category.

Suppose research says a demographic group is more likely to support a political party.

That can help explain elections.

It does not tell you how the individual standing beside you voted.

Suppose a crime statistic differs across groups.

That is a population-level fact worth understanding if the measurement is valid.

It is not probable cause against the next person you meet from the group.

This rule has to be symmetrical or it is worthless.

I do not want:

Stereotypes about my group are evil, but my stereotypes about your group are just pattern recognition.

Everybody suddenly becomes a Bayesian statistician when the prior insults somebody else.

No.

Same rule.

Sex.

Race.

Religion.

Class.

Profession.

Age.

Political identity.

Veteran.

Immigrant.

Academic.

Tradesman.

Group information can matter.

Individual evidence matters more as it accumulates.

And some stereotypes are simply wrong, exaggerated, outdated or built from terrible samples in the first place.

That is another reason the category does not get final authority.

I think this gives us a way out of one of the dumbest culture-war fights.

One camp sometimes talks as though noticing any group difference is moral corruption.

Another acts as though finding one statistical difference grants permission to treat every individual as an avatar of the average.

Both positions throw away information.

The first refuses useful population evidence.

The second refuses the actual person.

I want both.

Tell me the history.

Show me the statistics.

Explain the group pattern.

Then meet the fucking person.

Because the category does not know his grandfather.

It does not know her childhood.

It does not know what happened Tuesday.

It does not know whether this particular human being is the average, the exception or proof that your category is badly drawn.

The person gets veto power over the stereotype.

Not because population science is useless.

Because that is how you use population science without becoming an asshole.

BORING RECEIPT

This essay makes a reasoning and moral argument, not the claim that all stereotypes are statistically accurate. Some group-level differences are well supported; others are false, exaggerated, context dependent or outdated. Population averages cannot determine an individual value with certainty, and direct individual evidence should update prior expectations.

The principle is deliberately symmetrical across groups. It does not authorize discrimination, profiling or adverse treatment merely because a group-level statistic exists.

TITLE: I THINK I WAS COLLECTING EVIDENCE FROM A RIGGED EXPERIMENT
SUBTITLE: Sometimes the unfair rule you hate is also working in your favour.
SCHEDULE: November 3, 2026

I spent part of my life thinking I had discovered something unpleasant about women.

Sometimes two men could do basically the same thing and get very different reactions.

One guy says something.

Cold response.

I say something similar.

Smile.

Rule bends.

Conversation continues.

And part of my brain filed the result under:

Women are fucking unfair.

There was a problem with my experiment.

I may have been benefiting from the unfairness.

That is annoying.

I preferred the first interpretation.

In that version I was the scientist exposing a double standard.

In the second version I was also one of the treatment conditions.

I was bullied and called ugly when I was younger, and that old picture of myself hung around in my head much longer than it had any right to.

So when adult life repeatedly suggested some women found me attractive or charming, I did not naturally process that as:

Maybe appearance is changing how people respond to me.

I processed it as:

I guess I am good at talking to women.

Maybe I was.

But maybe that was not the only variable in the fucking room.

There is plenty of research showing attractiveness affects human judgment.

Not just dating.

People judged as more attractive are often evaluated more positively on various traits and can receive more favourable social treatment.

The effect is not universal.

It varies by outcome and context.

Attractiveness is not a halo that turns idiots into Nobel laureates.

But the bias is real enough that researchers have been studying it for decades.

A classic meta-analysis found attractive adults and children were generally judged and treated more positively than unattractive people.

An earlier meta-analysis found the “beautiful is good” effect was moderate overall and especially strong for judgments of social competence, while much weaker or near zero for traits like integrity and concern for others.

That qualification is important.

Humans do not simply think:

Hot = saint.

The bias has shape.

And in 2025, researchers found something else I love because it fits this story perfectly.

People noticed gender- and race-biased outcomes more readily than attractiveness-biased outcomes.

The attractiveness bias could sit in the result without people spontaneously seeing it.

Maybe I was one of those people.

I noticed when appearance seemed to hurt another man.

I was slower to notice when it helped me.

That is an excellent human trick.

We notice unfairness much faster from underneath it than from on top of it.

This is why I keep wanting self-implication inside the Clove project.

If I study gender bias only when it disadvantages men, I am not studying gender bias.

I am building a complaint department.

If I study institutional privilege only when somebody else has it, same problem.

The harder question is:

How much of what I thought I earned was partly somebody else's bias working in my favour?

I do not know.

And I am not going to rewrite my life as though charm, competence or effort suddenly did nothing.

That would be the opposite distortion.

The point is to add the missing variable.

Maybe I worked for something.

Maybe I was good at it.

Maybe I also walked into the room with an advantage I did not create.

Those can all be true.

That has implications far beyond looks.

Credentials.

Height.

Accent.

Class.

Uniform.

Race.

Sex.

Confidence.

Clothing.

The human brain is constantly receiving signals and mistaking them for substance.

Beauty becomes goodness.

Confidence becomes knowledge.

A degree becomes intelligence.

Followers become reputation.

AI fluency becomes understanding.

Sometimes the signal correlates with the thing.

Sometimes it does not.

And sometimes you benefit enough from the confusion that you stop asking.

So this is my correction.

Maybe some of the evidence I collected about other people's unfairness came from a rigged experiment.

And maybe I was standing on the good side of the rig.

Count that too.

BORING RECEIPT

This essay combines Ryan's lived interpretation with established research on physical-attractiveness bias. A 2000 Psychological Bulletin meta-analysis of 11 meta-analyses found that more attractive children and adults were generally judged and treated more positively. A 1991 meta-analysis found the “beauty is good” stereotype was moderate rather than universal, varying substantially by trait and context. A 2025 series of preregistered studies found people were less likely to spontaneously notice attractiveness-biased outcomes than comparable gender- or race-biased outcomes.

None of this proves why any particular person treated Ryan a particular way. The personal examples are recollections, not controlled experiments.

TITLE: DON'T JUST TELL A BOY HE MATTERS
SUBTITLE: Put him somewhere his contribution matters.
SCHEDULE: November 6, 2026

You matter.

You are enough.

Believe in yourself.

Great.

Now give the kid something to fucking do.

I am not against encouragement.

Kids need love.

They need people in their corner.

They need to know one bad grade, rejection, mistake or awkward phase does not make them disposable.

But I keep wondering whether we sometimes confuse being told you have value with experiencing yourself as valuable.

Those are not exactly the same thing.

Imagine a boy spends all day being told:

You matter.

Then nobody needs him for anything.

He has no responsibility.

No useful skill.

No team relying on him.

No younger kid watching him.

No adult trusting him with a real task.

No place where failure matters enough to require improvement.

What exactly is the message supposed to attach itself to?

Now imagine somebody says:

We need you here Saturday.

These boards have to be cut.

That younger kid needs a partner.

You are responsible for checking the gear.

The team is missing someone in your position.

Your grandmother needs the steps fixed.

Different message.

You did not merely hear that you matter.

Reality sent a receipt.

That is the hypothesis I want to build around boys.

Belonging may become more durable when it comes with contribution.

Competence may make confidence less dependent on applause.

Responsibility may give strength somewhere useful to go.

And mentorship may work partly because an adult is not merely complimenting a young person but bringing him into a relationship where expectations, support and real tasks exist.

The mentoring evidence is encouraging without being magical.

A large 2019 meta-analysis covering 70 youth-mentoring studies and more than 25,000 young people found statistically significant positive effects overall, while emphasizing that impacts were modest and programs varied.

Other reviews have found smaller or even negligible effects in some school-based programs.

That is important.

MENTORING WORKS is too simple.

Relationship quality matters.

Program design matters.

Badly supported or prematurely ended relationships can potentially hurt.

Again: no magic intervention.

This is why I do not want to launch a boys' program made of inspirational posters and good intentions.

I want to test environments.

Mentoring.

Martial arts.

Trades.

Sport.

Service.

Volunteering.

Building things.

Repairing things.

Cooking for people.

Helping younger kids.

Working with older men.

Father-son projects.

Teams where the boy has an actual job.

Maybe some of those do nothing.

Maybe different boys need completely different environments.

Maybe the mentor relationship matters far more than the activity.

Maybe belonging comes first and competence follows.

Maybe competence comes first and opens the door to belonging.

Those are research questions.

But the design principle feels worth testing:

Do not only give a boy a message about his worth.

Give him a path to evidence.

A board that fits because he measured it correctly.

A belt earned through practice.

A person who trusted him and was not disappointed.

A team that noticed when he did not show up.

A younger kid who learned something from him.

A mistake he repaired instead of being rescued from.

That is not “man up.”

It is not turning childhood into boot camp.

It is not saying love must be earned.

Love should not depend on performance.

Competence does.

That distinction matters.

A boy should know:

I am loved even when I fail.

And:

If I want to be good at this, I have to practice until I stop sucking.

Those two sentences can live in the same house.

Maybe healthy masculinity is not something we lecture into boys at all.

Maybe we build places where courage, strength, patience, service, tenderness, discipline and competence have real jobs to do.

Then we let the boy discover which kind of man he wants to become.

Don't just tell him he matters.

Put him somewhere his contribution matters.

Then shut up long enough for him to find out.

BORING RECEIPT

Youth mentoring has a real but non-magical evidence base. A 2019 meta-analysis of 70 intergenerational one-on-one mentoring studies covering 25,286 youth found a statistically significant positive effect across outcomes while stressing that program impacts remain modest. A meta-analysis of youth-initiated mentoring found a small-to-medium positive overall association. Other reviews, including school-based mentoring analyses, have found very small, inconsistent or null effects, showing that “mentoring” is not one uniform intervention.

The broader claim that responsibility, trades, martial arts, sport, service or building projects reliably produce healthy masculinity is not established here. Those are candidate environments for research, not proven prescriptions.
