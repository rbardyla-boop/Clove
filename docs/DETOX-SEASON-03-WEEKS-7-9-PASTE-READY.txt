TITLE: YOU DON'T GET TO KNOW ME WELL ENOUGH TO OPTIMIZE ME
SUBTITLE: Persuasion changes when the speaker can measure the listener.
SCHEDULE: October 6, 2026

Persuasion is old.

Really old.

Older than democracy.

Older than advertising.

Probably about six minutes younger than language.

One caveperson wanted the better sleeping spot and another caveperson had to explain why giving it up was actually a tremendous opportunity for personal growth.

Humans persuade.

That is not the new part.

The new part is measurement.

There is a difference between standing on a stage and saying:

Here is my argument.

And looking at ten thousand people individually and asking:

Which version of my argument is most likely to work on this one?

That is microtargeting in its most interesting form.

Not magic.

Not hypnosis.

Not a laser beam from Cambridge Analytica that enters your skull and changes your postal ballot while you sleep.

It is a feedback problem.

What do I know about you?

What can I infer?

What messages have worked on people who resemble you?

Which issue gets your attention?

Which source do you trust?

Which wording makes you move?

And how quickly can I learn from the answer?

That changes the relationship between speaker and listener.

If I stand in a town square, everybody hears the same pitch.

They can argue about it together.

A journalist can quote it.

My opponents can attack it.

You can compare what I told you with what I told your neighbour.

Personalized persuasion can fracture that common surface.

You get one message.

I get another.

A third person gets something neither of us saw.

That does not make the message false.

It does make inspection harder.

And this is where privacy and agency collide.

People often talk about privacy as though it means hiding embarrassing photographs or keeping your bank password secret.

That is part of it.

But there is another reason I might not want somebody building a detailed model of me:

I do not necessarily want to become easier to operate.

A shopkeeper knowing my shoe size is useful.

A campaign knowing which emotional frame is most likely to shift my opinion is a different relationship.

The funny part is that the measured persuasive advantage of microtargeting is nowhere near as settled as the horror stories imply.

Some experiments find an advantage.

A 2023 PNAS study found that a microtargeting strategy could outperform alternative messaging strategies under certain conditions.

But most of that advantage showed up on one of the two policy issues, and adding more targeting variables did not keep improving persuasion.

Then researchers gave GPT-4 demographic and political information about individual participants and let it produce personalized political messages.

The messages were persuasive.

The personalization was not the superpower.

In a preregistered experiment with 8,587 people, the microtargeted GPT-4 messages were not statistically more persuasive overall than the best generic messages.

That should make everybody uncomfortable in a more sophisticated way.

The scary part may not be:

The machine knows my exact psychological weak point.

It may be:

The machine can produce pretty good persuasive arguments for almost everyone, cheaply, endlessly and at scale.

Different problem.

Still worth caring about.

And Canada just gave us another useful reality check.

A 2026 study tested fifteen real campaign ads during the 2025 federal election across 3,346 respondents.

Political advertising could move candidate evaluations and vote intentions.

But the effects were very small and mostly homogeneous.

Again, that challenges the idea that every voter is a unique lock waiting for the right campaign key.

Good.

Kill the mythology where the evidence kills it.

But do not confuse “the sniper rifle metaphor is exaggerated” with “data-driven persuasion does not matter.”

The capability still matters.

The asymmetry still matters.

The lack of a shared public message can still matter.

The capacity to test thousands of variants cheaply can still matter.

So my rule is not:

Nobody may persuade me.

That would be impossible and stupid.

My rule is:

If you are measuring me in order to change me, I want to know enough about the machinery to decide whether I consent to the relationship.

Because persuasion is part of politics.

Invisible optimization does not have to be.

BORING RECEIPT

A 2023 PNAS study found that political microtargeting could outperform alternative messaging strategies under some conditions, but the advantage was context dependent, concentrated largely in one issue, and did not increase simply by adding more targeting variables.

A 2024 PNAS experiment with 8,587 participants found GPT-4 political messages were persuasive, but personalized microtargeting did not significantly outperform the best non-targeted messages overall.

A 2026 Canadian Journal of Political Science study tested 15 campaign advertisements during the 2025 federal election on 3,346 respondents. Ads could shift evaluations and vote intentions, but effects were very small and mostly homogeneous, challenging strong claims about large microtargeting advantages.

TITLE: MICROTARGETING IS NOT A SNIPER RIFLE
SUBTITLE: The mythology may be scarier than the measured persuasion effect.
SCHEDULE: October 9, 2026

Cambridge Analytica received one hell of a marketing campaign.

Not necessarily for its clients.

For Cambridge Analytica.

By the time the scandal had finished travelling around the world, political microtargeting sounded like somebody had invented a psychological sniper rifle.

Feed it enough Facebook data and somewhere in a dark room a consultant could apparently locate the exact childhood wound required to make you vote for municipal drainage reform.

I am exaggerating.

But not by much compared with some of the mythology.

The real research is less cinematic.

That does not mean harmless.

It means complicated.

A useful definition first.

Microtargeting means using information about people to decide which message to show which people.

The information can be simple.

Age.

Region.

Party identification.

Issue position.

Or potentially much richer.

The fantasy is that every additional data point makes the persuasive weapon more precise.

The experiments do not give us that clean story.

In 2023, researchers combined machine learning with message pretesting and found a microtargeted strategy could outperform other approaches in one experiment.

That matters.

Microtargeting can work.

But there were two giant asterisks.

The advantage was driven mainly by one of the two policy issues.

And targeting on more than one characteristic did not produce additional gains.

Then generative AI arrived and everybody understandably asked:

Oh good. What happens when the machine can write a different political message for every human being alive?

A 2024 preregistered experiment actually tested a version of that.

GPT-4 generated political messages using up to nine personal attributes.

The messages moved attitudes.

But the microtargeted messages did not significantly beat the best generic messages overall.

One attribute versus nine?

No meaningful aggregate advantage.

A separate set of AI persuasion experiments published in 2025 found something similar: customized and interactive AI messages were persuasive, but not clearly more persuasive than a single generic message.

Then Canada supplied a nice little kick in the teeth to the superweapon story.

Researchers tested fifteen ads from the 2025 federal election.

They found effects.

Very small ones.

And the effects were mostly similar across different kinds of people.

If persuasion effects are mostly homogeneous, there is less hidden gold for the microtargeter to mine.

So can we stop caring?

No.

Here is where the pendulum usually launches through the wall.

One side says:

MICROTARGETING CAN CONTROL ELECTIONS.

The other hears the effect sizes and says:

See? Nothing burger.

Neither follows.

Small effects can matter in close elections.

Cheap messages can be sent at enormous scale.

Targeting can be useful for turnout, fundraising, volunteer recruitment and message allocation even if it does not convert human beings like a video-game cheat code.

And the privacy issue exists even when the persuasion effect is zero.

If an organization builds a behavioural model of you and fails to persuade you, it has still built a behavioural model of you.

That is a separate question.

This is why I want to retire the sniper-rifle metaphor.

It makes the technology sound more powerful than the evidence says.

And ironically that can make us miss the boring problems that are actually real.

Data accumulation.

Opacity.

Different people receiving different promises.

Automated message generation.

Huge numbers of cheap experiments.

Difficulty auditing what was shown to whom.

The ability to optimize for something other than truth.

You do not need mind control to create an agency problem.

You just need the person doing the persuading to understand the machinery better than the person being persuaded.

BORING RECEIPT

The empirical literature does not support a simple verdict that political microtargeting either never works or functions as an extraordinarily precise persuasion weapon. Tappin and colleagues found meaningful microtargeting gains under some conditions in 2023, but those gains were issue dependent and did not grow by simply targeting more variables. Hackenburg and Margetts found in 2024 that GPT-4 messages were persuasive but microtargeted messages did not significantly outperform the best generic messages overall. A 2026 Canadian replication found political-ad effects during the 2025 federal election were very small and mostly homogeneous across respondents.

TITLE: THE ALGORITHM DIDN'T BRAINWASH YOU
SUBTITLE: That does not mean the ranking system is neutral.
SCHEDULE: October 13, 2026

I need everybody to put down their favourite cartoon version of the algorithm for a minute.

One cartoon says:

THE ALGORITHM BRAINWASHED THE COUNTRY.

The other says:

People choose what they watch. The algorithm is irrelevant.

I would like to introduce a third possibility.

The machine matters.

The human matters.

And neither gets to fire the other one from the investigation.

Recommendation systems decide what to put near your eyeballs.

Humans decide what to click, ignore, believe, laugh at, hate-watch, search for and come back to.

Those two systems interact.

That feedback loop is the story.

Take YouTube.

The company openly says recommendations use signals including watch history, search history, subscriptions, likes, dislikes, “not interested” feedback and satisfaction surveys.

That means the feed is not neutral in the ordinary sense of the word.

It is ranked.

Personalized.

Designed to predict what you may want next.

But “ranked” does not mean “mind control.”

In 2025, researchers ran four naturalistic YouTube experiments with 8,883 participants.

They deliberately manipulated recommendations to create more partisan filter bubbles and rabbit-hole-like conditions.

The result?

No detectable short-term polarizing effect on participants' attitudes.

That is a major problem for the lazy story that a few partisan recommendations automatically turn normal people into political goblins.

The researchers were careful, though.

They could not rule out long-term effects.

They could not rule out effects on smaller vulnerable groups.

And the experiment did not prove recommendation systems are harmless.

Then another experiment landed in Science in late 2025.

Researchers changed the ranking of real X feeds during the 2024 U.S. election period.

They increased or decreased exposure to posts expressing antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity.

That reranking moved out-party animosity by a measurable amount over the experiment.

So which paper wins?

Neither.

They asked different questions in different environments.

That is exactly why “the algorithm” is such a useless monster word.

Which algorithm?

Ranking what?

For which people?

For how long?

Toward what outcome?

News exposure?

Partisan hostility?

Shopping?

Body image?

Conspiracy belief?

Watch time?

You cannot take one effect and staple it to every platform.

What we can say safely is more interesting anyway.

Ranking changes exposure.

Exposure can sometimes change attitudes or emotion.

People also actively choose content.

Effects vary.

That means the human is not a helpless sponge.

And the ranking system is not a neutral pipe.

Good.

Now we have something worth studying.

This also gives us a better digital-temperance rule.

Do not ask:

Did the algorithm make me think this?

You probably cannot answer that cleanly.

Ask:

What has this system been repeatedly putting in front of me?

What have I repeatedly rewarded it for showing me?

What did I search for first?

What happens if I deliberately change the signals?

What happens if I leave the feed and seek the information directly?

That gives agency back to the user without pretending the environment is irrelevant.

The machine chooses from the menu.

You keep teaching it what kind of restaurant you are willing to eat in.

Sometimes both of you have terrible taste.

BORING RECEIPT

YouTube states that its recommendation system uses signals including watch and search history, subscriptions, likes, dislikes, feedback and satisfaction surveys.

A 2025 PNAS study ran four naturalistic recommendation experiments with 8,883 participants and found no detectable short-term polarizing effects from more partisan YouTube recommendations, while explicitly noting that long-term effects or effects on vulnerable subgroups could not be ruled out.

A 2025 Science field experiment with 1,256 X users found that experimentally increasing or decreasing exposure to antidemocratic/partisan-animosity content altered out-party animosity, providing causal evidence that feed composition can affect affective polarization in that setting.

These studies do not justify a universal claim that algorithms either cause or do not cause polarization.

TITLE: THE WORST FIVE MINUTES
SUBTITLE: What if your feed learns from the version of you that should never have been promoted to management?
SCHEDULE: October 16, 2026

I have a theory I do not yet know how to prove.

That sentence is important.

This is a hypothesis.

Not a finding.

Here it is:

What if personalization occasionally learns too much from a temporary version of you?

Not the real you.

Not the fake you.

The five-minute you.

Angry you.

Horny you.

Lonely you.

Scared you.

Insomniac-at-2:17-a.m. you.

The version of you who should not be allowed to choose a tattoo, write to your ex or restructure national monetary policy.

Call him Temporary Regional Manager Ryan.

He has authority.

He should not.

Recommendation systems learn from behaviour.

YouTube, for example, says watch history, searches, likes, dislikes and other signals influence future recommendations. It even tells users that deleting watch history can prevent those watched videos from shaping future recommendations.

That is not secret.

It is the product working as designed.

Usually useful.

You watch woodworking videos.

It finds better woodworking videos.

Great.

But humans do not have one stable interest state.

Sometimes curiosity is temporary.

You watch three videos about a frightening medical symptom because your knee hurts.

You spend twenty minutes reading about divorce because you had a fight.

You fall down a political rabbit hole because somebody pissed you off at lunch.

You click looksmaxxing videos after a rejection.

The system sees behaviour.

It does not necessarily know whether the behaviour represents:

A durable preference.

A passing mood.

Research.

Morbid curiosity.

A joke.

A crisis.

Or the intellectual equivalent of eating an entire bag of chips in the parking lot.

Now imagine that temporary burst influencing what appears next.

Then the new recommendations generate more behaviour.

Then that behaviour becomes more signal.

I am not claiming this reliably traps people.

I do not have evidence for that causal chain at population scale.

The YouTube experiments I wrote about earlier actually caution against grand claims about short-term algorithmic radicalization.

Good.

Keep the caution.

But the design question remains interesting:

How well can a personalization system distinguish preference from state?

And should users have an easier way to tell it:

That was not me joining a religion. I was having a weird fucking Tuesday.

Some platforms already provide crude controls.

Delete history.

Pause history.

Mark something “not interested.”

Reset recommendations.

Those tools imply something useful: the profile is editable.

Digital stewardship could teach people to use that deliberately.

Maybe after a period of intense emotion, clear the trail.

Maybe research sensitive subjects in a mode that does not feed future recommendations.

Maybe periodically inspect what the system thinks you like.

Maybe stop treating every click as a democratic vote from your eternal soul.

I want this studied properly.

Give participants a temporary emotional or situational interest.

Measure what the recommender learns.

Measure how long the effect lasts.

Test whether people notice.

Test whether controls actually reset it.

Test whether vulnerable users are different from everybody else.

Until then, the honest label is:

Plausible mechanism.

Unproven harm.

Worth testing.

That is less sexy than announcing the algorithm has stolen our souls.

It is also how you avoid becoming full of shit.

BORING RECEIPT

YouTube publicly states that watch history, search history and other interaction signals influence recommendations. It also states that deleting or turning off watch history can change or remove history-based recommendations.

This essay's central claim — that a short-lived emotional state may sometimes be overlearned by a personalization system and then reinforced — is a hypothesis. The sources establish that behaviour influences recommendations; they do not establish the proposed transient-state feedback mechanism or a resulting health/political harm.

TITLE: THE WEF CLIP WAS REAL. THE MATH WASN'T.
SUBTITLE: A real quote can still support a bullshit conclusion.
SCHEDULE: October 20, 2026

I hate when fact-checking works like this:

Person A:

Here is a disturbing video.

Person B:

That's a conspiracy theory.

Person A:

But did you watch the fucking video?

Person B:

CONSPIRACY THEORY.

Congratulations.

You have just converted a questionable inference into a lifelong hobby.

So let us do the World Economic Forum clip properly.

The clip is real.

On September 20, 2017, Klaus Schwab spoke at Harvard Kennedy School's Institute of Politics during the Malcolm H. Wiener Lecture on International Political Economy.

Harvard still hosts the event page.

The Institute of Politics still has the original video on its YouTube channel.

During the discussion, Schwab talked about the World Economic Forum's Young Global Leaders network and said they “penetrate the cabinets.”

He then said that half of Justin Trudeau's cabinet, or even more than half, were Young Global Leaders.

That happened.

You do not need a guy in sunglasses whispering over dramatic music.

Harvard uploaded the fucking video.

Now comes the part people keep skipping.

What does that prove?

It proves Klaus Schwab made the statement.

It proves the WEF deliberately built a leadership network and takes pride when alumni reach powerful positions.

It proves there were real Canadian cabinet links to the program.

For example, the WEF's own records identify Mélanie Joly as a 2016 Young Global Leader and Chrystia Freeland as a Young Global Leader alumna. WEF material also identifies Scott Brison as part of that community.

Those are receipts.

What it does not prove is that Klaus Schwab controlled the Canadian government.

Membership in a leadership network is not mind control.

Attendance at Davos is not a loyalty oath.

Shared policy preferences are not proof of command.

And Schwab's “half the cabinet” arithmetic is a claim that requires its own evidence.

I have not found a primary-source roster capable of substantiating that number for the 2017 Trudeau cabinet.

Several people clearly had WEF/YGL ties.

“More than half” is a much larger claim.

This is exactly why digital archaeology matters.

When somebody says the evidence was deleted, do not stop at today's website.

Look for old PDFs.

Archived rosters.

Annual reports.

Event programs.

Wayback snapshots.

Contemporaneous news.

The internet forgets badly.

But the opposite mistake is just as dangerous.

Finding one authentic artifact does not validate the entire theory built around it.

Real clip.

Real network.

Real elite access.

Unproven control.

Unverified cabinet fraction.

Those can all be true at the same time.

This is the problem with the words “debunked” and “confirmed.”

Reality is often annoyingly itemized.

You have to audit each fucking line.

The skeptic who says the clip is fake loses credibility.

The conspiracy theorist who says the clip proves Schwab runs Canada also outruns the receipt.

So here is the rule I want Clove to use forever:

Never let somebody else's exaggeration force you into the opposite exaggeration.

If the embarrassing evidence is real, say it is real.

If the scary conclusion is not demonstrated, say that too.

The clip was real.

The control theory does not automatically follow.

And the math still needs a receipt.

BORING RECEIPT

Harvard Kennedy School and the Harvard Institute of Politics document Klaus Schwab's September 20, 2017 lecture, “Strengthening Collaboration in a Fractured World.” The Institute of Politics' original video remains online. In the discussion, Schwab used the phrase “we penetrate the cabinets” while discussing the Young Global Leaders network and claimed that half or more of Trudeau's cabinet were YGLs.

WEF primary material confirms at least some Canadian cabinet connections. Its 2016 announcement identifies Mélanie Joly as a Young Global Leader, a 2017 WEF program identifies Chrystia Freeland as a Young Global Leader alumna, and WEF YGL material identifies Scott Brison in the community.

Those facts establish network participation and Schwab's boast. They do not establish that the WEF controlled those politicians, dictated government policy, or that “more than half” of Trudeau's 2017 cabinet can be verified from a complete primary-source roster. Treat the cabinet fraction as Schwab's claim unless and until the roster math is independently reconstructed.

TITLE: GOVERNMENT LIES. THAT DOESN'T MAKE EVERY CONSPIRACY TRUE.
SUBTITLE: Institutional dishonesty destroys trust. It does not repeal the burden of proof.
SCHEDULE: October 23, 2026

Here is a sentence adults should be able to hold without spraining anything:

Governments have lied.

Not theoretically.

Not because my cousin found a Telegram screenshot.

Documented.

Archived.

Admitted.

And somehow the internet has turned that fact into two equally stupid positions.

Position one:

The government would never do that.

Position two:

The government did that once, therefore the lizard people probably own Saskatchewan.

No.

History earns skepticism.

It does not eliminate evidence.

Take Watergate.

The U.S. National Archives states plainly that after the 1972 break-in, President Nixon orchestrated a cover-up designed to block the investigation.

That is not a conspiracy theory.

It was a conspiracy.

There are tapes.

Records.

Court cases.

Impeachment proceedings.

A resignation.

Take the U.S. Public Health Service study at Tuskegee.

The CDC now says participants did not give informed consent, were told they were being treated for “bad blood,” and were not offered penicillin after it became the standard treatment for syphilis.

In 1997, President Bill Clinton apologized on behalf of the federal government and called what happened shameful.

Again:

Real.

Documented.

Government institutions can betray trust.

That matters because trust is not an infinite resource.

Every lie, cover-up and concealed abuse writes an IOU that some future public servant may be forced to pay.

Then a new crisis arrives.

The government says:

Trust us.

And somebody in the back row says:

Why?

That question did not come from nowhere.

The mistake happens on the next line.

Because Watergate was real, my theory about today is true.

No.

Because Tuskegee was real, every medical institution is lying now.

No.

Because intelligence agencies concealed something before, every classified program somebody imagines exists.

No.

Past deception changes the prior.

It does not produce the verdict.

If somebody has lied to you repeatedly, it is rational to demand better evidence from them next time.

It is not rational to believe every accusation made against them.

Those are different standards.

This distinction is vital because bad fact-checking creates conspiracy theorists.

If an institution responds to a partly true claim by denying the embarrassing true part, people notice.

Then when the institution correctly challenges the unsupported part, nobody listens.

Trust is gone.

The better response is itemized honesty.

Yes, this document is authentic.

No, it does not prove that conclusion.

Yes, the government concealed this.

No, that does not establish your separate allegation.

Yes, the official explanation changed.

Here is why.

Here is the evidence.

Here is what remains unknown.

That is how trust should be rebuilt.

Not:

Believe authority.

Not:

Distrust authority.

Inspect authority.

And apply the same rule to the guy accusing authority.

Because skepticism without standards becomes gullibility wearing combat boots.

BORING RECEIPT

The U.S. National Archives states that President Richard Nixon orchestrated a cover-up after the Watergate break-in and that tapes later revealed his knowledge of the cover-up. The CDC's official history of the U.S. Public Health Service study at Tuskegee states that informed consent was not collected, participants were told they were being treated for “bad blood,” and penicillin was not offered after it became the treatment of choice. President Bill Clinton formally apologized in 1997, calling the government's conduct wrong and shameful.

These documented cases justify the proposition that governments and public institutions are capable of deception and serious abuse. They do not validate unrelated allegations. Each new claim still requires evidence specific to that claim.
