# CloveLearn v2 — Brotherhood Without an Enemy Research Ledger

Status: **F0 / PROVISIONAL EVIDENCE LEDGER**  
Research cutoff: **2026-08-12**  
Initial product population: **men aged 18–24**  
No intervention with minors is authorized by this document.

## Governing claim discipline

This ledger is deliberately stricter than a persuasive essay.

Every program idea is classified as one of:

- **ESTABLISHED** — supported by multiple strong studies/reviews for the stated narrow claim.
- **PROBABLE** — evidence is supportive but causal inference, transferability, or precision is limited.
- **CONTESTED** — credible evidence points in materially different directions.
- **HYPOTHESIS** — plausible synthesis of supported components but not directly established.
- **UNSUPPORTED / RETIRE** — the current formulation outruns available evidence.

`ESTABLISHED` never means universal, deterministic, or large-effect.

## Core Clove v2 hypothesis

> For directionless or socially disconnected young adult men, a structured loop of real-world challenge, competence, service, reliable human connection, reflection, and increasing responsibility can improve belonging, agency and purpose more effectively than passive consumption of advice alone.

**Classification:** `HYPOTHESIS`.

No study located directly tests this full package, this exact population, or the proposed Clove delivery model. The project must therefore test the mechanism rather than advertise it as proven.

# 1. The population problem

## Claim 1.1 — Young adulthood is a low-belonging period in Canada

**Classification:** `ESTABLISHED DESCRIPTIVE FINDING`

Statistics Canada analysis of the 2021–2024 Canadian Social Survey reports strong local-community belonging for:

- age 15–19: 63.1%
- age 20–24: 46.8%
- age 25–29: 42.5%

This provides a strong Canadian reason to examine the transition into early adulthood. It does **not** show that weak belonging causes mental illness or that an intervention will fix it.

**Source:** Statistics Canada, *Community and well-being: Exploring sense of belonging among youth* (2025).  
https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/75-006-x/2025003/article/00001-eng.htm

## Claim 1.2 — Young Canadian men are uniquely less connected than young Canadian women

**Classification:** `UNSUPPORTED / DO NOT CLAIM`

The same Statistics Canada analysis reports only a small overall gender difference in strong local belonging (men 50.5%, women 51.8%) and does not establish a unique male belonging collapse.

**Program implication:** Clove may deliberately design for men because the intervention, language and recruitment strategy are male-focused. It must not justify that choice with an unsupported claim that Canadian young men are categorically lonelier or less connected than young women.

## Claim 1.3 — Strong belonging is associated with better wellbeing

**Classification:** `ESTABLISHED ASSOCIATION; CAUSALITY NOT ESTABLISHED`

Statistics Canada reports strong relationships between community belonging and positive mental-health/wellbeing indicators among youth and young adults. The design is observational.

**Program implication:** belonging is a valid outcome to measure, not a guaranteed causal lever.

# 2. Social-connection interventions in the actual 18–24 target age

## Claim 2.1 — Deliberate social-connection interventions can improve some young-adult outcomes

**Classification:** `PROBABLE / LIMITED EVIDENCE BASE`

A systematic review/meta-analysis specifically restricted to general-population young adults aged 18–24 found only six eligible studies. Excluding studies at serious risk of bias, social-connection interventions produced a small pooled reduction in depression (`SMD -0.19`, 95% CI `-0.33 to -0.05`). Pooled loneliness reduction was not statistically significant (`SMD -0.10`, 95% CI `-0.24 to 0.05`).

This is a critical calibration point: **social connection is important, but simply creating a social intervention does not reliably eliminate loneliness.**

**Source:** Vazquez Alvarez C, et al. *Social connection interventions and depression in young adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Soc Psychiatry Psychiatr Epidemiol. 2025;60:549–562. PMID 39150513. DOI 10.1007/s00127-024-02722-1.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39150513/

**Clove implication:** Mission participation, return, belonging and real-world relationship quality must all be measured separately. “Joined a group” is not an adequate success endpoint.

# 3. Mentoring

## Claim 3.1 — One-to-one youth mentoring produces positive but modest average outcomes

**Classification:** `ESTABLISHED FOR YOUTH; TRANSFER TO 18–24 IS PROBABLE, NOT ESTABLISHED`

Raposa et al. synthesized 70 one-to-one intergenerational mentoring studies covering 25,286 youth (average age about 12). The overall effect was statistically significant but modest; later follow-up synthesis reports the Raposa estimate as Hedges' `g = 0.21` (95% CI `0.14–0.28`). Moderators in the original study included a larger proportion of male youth, more male mentors/helping-profession mentors, shorter meetings, and use of youth self-report.

**Primary source:** Raposa EB, et al. *The Effects of Youth Mentoring Programs: A Meta-analysis of Outcome Studies.* J Youth Adolesc. 2019;48:423–443. PMID 30661211. DOI 10.1007/s10964-019-00982-8.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30661211/

**Important boundary:** the mean participant age was far below Clove's proposed first population. We may borrow mechanisms and safeguards, not assume the same effect size for men 18–24.

## Claim 3.2 — Mentoring quality and relationship handling matter; a mentor is not automatically beneficial

**Classification:** `ESTABLISHED CAUTION`

In a national randomized school-based mentoring study of 1,139 youth, intact mentoring relationships were associated with academic improvement; prematurely terminated relationships showed no impact, and youth rematched after termination showed negative academic impacts.

**Source:** Grossman JB, et al. *The test of time in school-based mentoring: the role of relationship duration and re-matching on academic outcomes.* Am J Community Psychol. 2012. PMID 21626084.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21626084/

MENTOR's 2025 Fifth Edition standards explicitly include program design, mentor screening, participant preparation, mentor training, relationship support, caregiver/community engagement, managed endings and evaluation.

**Practice source:** MENTOR, *Elements of Effective Practice for Mentoring*, Fifth Edition.  
https://eepm.mentoring.org/

**Clove implication:** Clove v0.1 should **not** open a marketplace where unknown adults privately message vulnerable young users. The first test can use missions without one-to-one mentor matching. If human mentoring is later introduced, screening, supervision, boundaries, incident handling and managed endings are product requirements, not optional policy text.

## Claim 3.3 — Putting higher-risk young people together necessarily makes them worse through “deviancy training”

**Classification:** `CONTESTED / DO NOT STATE AS GENERAL FACT`

Peer contagion is a plausible and documented concern in some contexts. However, a 2005 review found 17 of 18 new meta-analytic tests did not support broad claims of iatrogenic/deviancy-training effects from group treatment of antisocial youth.

**Source:** Weiss B, et al. *Iatrogenic effects of group treatment for antisocial youths.* J Consult Clin Psychol. 2005;73:1036–1044. PMID 16392977. DOI 10.1037/0022-006X.73.6.1036.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16392977/

**Clove implication:** treat negative peer reinforcement as a design risk to monitor; do not use it as a blanket argument against groups.

# 4. Service / volunteering

## Claim 4.1 — Volunteering is associated with higher flourishing in adolescents

**Classification:** `PROBABLE ASSOCIATION; CAUSAL BENEFIT UNPROVEN`

A 2023 cross-sectional US study of 51,895 children/adolescents found adolescent volunteering associated with higher parent-reported flourishing (`aOR 1.97`, 95% CI `1.72–2.26`), better reported health, lower anxiety odds and lower behavioral-problem odds. The depression association was not statistically significant. The design cannot establish that volunteering caused the differences.

**Source:** Lanza K, et al. *Volunteering, Health, and Well-being of Children and Adolescents in the United States.* JAMA Netw Open. 2023;6:e2315980. PMID 37252742. DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.15980.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37252742/

## Claim 4.2 — Service will automatically create meaning for young adults

**Classification:** `UNSUPPORTED AS A UNIVERSAL CLAIM`

Evidence in emerging adults indicates that the relationship between volunteering and purpose can depend on motivation. Service that is compulsory, performative or poorly matched may not produce the same psychological effect as genuinely prosocial action.

**Clove implication:** Mission 001 should measure whether the user experienced usefulness/meaning rather than assuming it. Service should be one route, not the only route.

# 5. Trades / apprenticeship / competence

## Claim 5.1 — Completing apprenticeship is associated with stronger employment and earnings outcomes

**Classification:** `PROBABLE / STRONG DESCRIPTIVE ADMINISTRATIVE EVIDENCE`

Employment and Social Development Canada's evaluation reports that apprenticeship completers/certified apprentices have stronger employment and earnings outcomes than discontinuers. The evaluation explicitly states that methodological limitations prevent causal attribution of progression/certification differences to the Apprenticeship Grants themselves.

**Source:** Employment and Social Development Canada, *Apprenticeship Grants: Evaluation Report* (2024/2025 evaluation).  
https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/corporate/reports/evaluations/apprenticeship-grants-impact.html

A prior federal evaluation reported permanent employment in 80.8% of completers vs 77.0% of discontinuers and higher average annual income for completers ($69,512 vs $59,782), again as observational program outcome data.

**Source:** Employment and Social Development Canada, *Evaluation of the Apprenticeship Grants*.  
https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/corporate/reports/evaluations/apprenticeship-grants.html

## Claim 5.2 — Trades/apprenticeship are proven loneliness or purpose interventions

**Classification:** `UNSUPPORTED`

The evidence located supports labor-market and completion outcomes, not Clove's proposed psychosocial mechanism.

**Clove implication:** skill/trade missions are justified as **real competence-building opportunities**. Any claim that they improve belonging/purpose must be measured separately.

# 6. Sport / martial arts / physical challenge

## Claim 6.1 — Sport reliably reduces adolescent aggression

**Classification:** `CONTESTED`

Two 2025 meta-analyses reach different pooled conclusions:

1. Wang et al., 11 studies / 1,811 participants: no significant overall effect on aggression (`g 0.46`, CI `-0.24 to 1.16`) or anger; significant modest reduction in hostility (`g 0.29`, CI `0.13–0.45`); non-contact sport subgroup reduced anger.
2. Zhao & Wang, 18 studies / 2,479 participants: significant pooled reduction in overall aggression (`SMD -0.55`, CI `-0.89 to -0.21`) with substantial variation by intervention and population.

**Sources:**

- Wang H, et al. *Impact of sports interventions on aggressive behavior among adolescents: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Front Psychol. 2025. PMID 41280181. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1697324.  
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41280181/
- Zhao H, Wang Y. *Effects of physical exercise on aggressive behavior in children and adolescents: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* BMC Public Health. 2025. PMID 41250097. DOI 10.1186/s12889-025-25267-7.  
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41250097/

**Clove implication:** do not market sport as a proven anti-violence intervention. Physical challenge can still be tested as a route to mastery, routine, embodied effort and shared activity.

## Claim 6.2 — Martial arts specifically creates prosocial behavior and reduced aggression with a settled medium effect

**Classification:** `RESEARCH REQUIRED / DOWNGRADE FROM INPUT REPORT`

The initial Brotherhood report stated a stronger martial-arts-specific effect than this audit could independently reproduce from a strong directly matching review. The claim is not authorized for Clove copy until the exact source and outcome estimates are replayed.

**Clove implication:** martial arts remains a plausible mission/path partner because it offers externally tested skill progression and structured practice, not because Clove has proven a particular psychological effect size.

# 7. Purpose / meaning

## Claim 7.1 — Purpose can be cultivated; it is not simply a fixed trait

**Classification:** `PROBABLE`

A 2024 systematic review located 25 empirical studies examining determinants and educational interventions intended to cultivate young adults' life purpose. The literature is heterogeneous and does not identify one universal intervention recipe.

**Source:** Zhu M, Zhang W, Jiang F. *How to influence and cultivate young adults' life purpose in the process of education: a systematic review of empirical studies.* BMC Psychol. 2024;12:554. PMID 39407292. DOI 10.1186/s40359-024-02003-1.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39407292/

**Clove implication:** purpose should be treated as an outcome/process that can change, but “Clove gives you purpose” is too strong. The product can instead help users choose a valued direction and accumulate evidence of action in that direction.

# 8. Rites of passage / earned transition

## Claim 8.1 — Modern rites-of-passage programs for boys have promising evidence

**Classification:** `HYPOTHESIS / EARLY EVIDENCE`

A 2021 scoping review found only nine key articles on school-based rites-of-passage programs for adolescent boys and concluded that programs **may** improve community engagement, responsible citizenship and self-perception/positive identity. This is a sparse evidence base, mainly younger than Clove's initial population.

**Source:** Kingsman J. *Rites of Passage Programs for Adolescent Boys in Schools: A Scoping Review.* Boyhood Studies. 2021;14(2):90–115. DOI 10.3167/bhs.2021.140206.

A 2026 controlled pre/post study of 77 boys aged 11–14 in a 10-week counselor-facilitated rite-of-passage group found a between-group improvement in interpersonal difficulties (`d=0.69`) and a suggestive inappropriate-behavior effect requiring replication.

**Source:** Lessor et al. *Effects of a Counselor-Facilitated Rite of Passage Group on Behavioral Outcomes in At-Risk Adolescent Boys: Evidence From the Quest Project.* Journal of Adolescence. 2026. DOI 10.1002/jad.70208.

**Clove implication:** “earned transition” is a design inspiration, not a validated mechanism. Do not manufacture ceremonial rank before real-world behavior exists to justify it.

# 9. Positive masculinity / male-specific framing

## Claim 9.1 — Healthy male-focused framing can emphasize connection, motivation and authenticity

**Classification:** `THEORETICAL SUPPORT; OUTCOME EFFECT UNPROVEN`

A 2021 theoretical synthesis proposed a positive-masculinity framework for boys and young men centered on connection, motivation, authenticity and positive identity development. It is a framework for future interventions, not evidence that one particular male-branded program works.

**Source:** Kiselica et al. *Operationalizing positive masculinity: a theoretical synthesis and school-based framework to engage boys and young men.* Health Promot Int. 2021. PMID 33842967. DOI 10.1093/heapro/daab031.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33842967/

**Clove implication:** avoid defining a “real man.” Operationalize behaviors instead: reliability, competence, service, responsibility, emotional regulation, repair, learning, and helping the next person.

# 10. Peer support

## Claim 10.1 — Peer support is broadly proven to improve young people's mental health

**Classification:** `MIXED / DO NOT OVERSELL`

A 2025 systematic review of 32 controlled youth/young-adult peer-support studies found the most consistent support in eating disorders; results in depression/anxiety, suicidality, serious mental illness, health promotion and other domains were mixed. Training manuals, fidelity measures and outcome consistency were often missing.

**Source:** Hoagwood K, et al. *Effectiveness of Youth and Young Adult Peer Support in Mental Health Services: A Systematic Review.* Adm Policy Ment Health. 2025. PMID 40569353. DOI 10.1007/s10488-025-01451-0.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40569353/

A 2026 scoping review likewise describes the evidence for online peer mental-health support for people up to age 25 as limited and heterogeneous.

**Source:** Yuan S, et al. *Effectiveness and Experiences of Online Mental Health Peer Support for Young People: Systematic Scoping Review.* JMIR. 2026. PMID 41740145. DOI 10.2196/83139.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41740145/

**Clove implication:** a Clove community is not automatically therapeutic. The first pilot should avoid promising peer mental-health treatment and should measure adverse interactions as well as positive connection.

# 11. Intervention harm / overreach

## Claim 11.1 — Well-intended psychosocial programs can cause harm or produce null effects

**Classification:** `ESTABLISHED DESIGN PRINCIPLE`

A 2025 scoping review specifically examined potential harms in school-based group mental-health interventions and concluded that harms/adverse events require deliberate examination rather than assuming benign effects.

**Source:** Guzman-Holst C, et al. *Scoping review: potential harm from school-based group mental health interventions.* Child Adolesc Ment Health. 2025. PMID 40101758. DOI 10.1111/camh.12760.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40101758/

**Clove implication:** the evaluation plan needs negative outcomes: increased loneliness, shame, pressure, social conflict, dependency, dropout after failed missions, injury, financial strain, harassment, exclusion and worsening distress.

# 12. Corrections to the initial Brotherhood research input

The initial “Brotherhood Without an Enemy: Interventions for Male Youth” report is useful as a hypothesis map, but its evidence labels are too generous in several places.

| Input claim | F0 ruling | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Mentoring `g≈0.21` is beneficial | **SUPPORTED WITH LIMIT** | Strong youth meta-analysis; effect modest and younger sample than Clove target. |
| Longer mentoring relationships do not matter | **REJECT SIMPLIFICATION** | Duration alone is not the whole mechanism, but premature termination/rematching can matter and relationship handling is important. |
| Martial arts/sports clearly reduce aggression | **DOWNGRADE TO CONTESTED** | 2025 meta-analyses disagree on pooled aggression effects. |
| Volunteering produces ~66% higher flourishing | **CORRECT THE STATISTIC** | Adolescent aOR was 1.97, i.e. 97% higher *odds*, not “66% higher flourishing”; observational, not causal. |
| Trades/apprenticeship gives ~90% employment retention | **DO NOT CARRY FORWARD** | Canadian sources located support employment/earnings advantages but not that exact generalized figure or psychosocial mechanism. |
| Rites of passage improve identity/belonging | **HYPOTHESIS / EARLY EVIDENCE** | Small and mostly adolescent evidence base; 2026 controlled study is encouraging but narrow. |
| Belonging is important | **SUPPORTED AS ASSOCIATION** | Strong Canadian descriptive association; intervention causality remains separate. |
| Grouping at-risk youth can cause deviancy training | **CONTESTED** | Risk is plausible/context-dependent; broad iatrogenic claim did not survive later meta-analytic review. |
| Peer groups are generally beneficial | **DOWNGRADE TO MIXED/PROBABLE** | Effects depend on domain, implementation, training and fidelity. |
| Young males specifically face a unique belonging deficit | **NOT SUPPORTED BY CURRENT CANADIAN DATA** | Age effect is clear; aggregate gender difference is small. |

# 13. What the evidence actually supports building first

The evidence does **not** justify building a giant mentoring social network.

It supports a much narrower first experiment:

## `MISSION 001 — MAKE YOURSELF USEFUL`

For men aged 18–24 only in the first external pilot.

The participant chooses one externally testable route:

- **FIX** — diagnose and repair something real.
- **SERVE** — perform bounded useful service for another person/community organization.
- **LEARN** — acquire and demonstrate a concrete skill.
- **BUILD** — create something another person can actually use.

Clove provides:

1. a purpose/values selection step;
2. a bounded mission contract;
3. a next-action decomposition if blocked;
4. optional reality-check/reset tools;
5. an after-action review;
6. a locally held completion record;
7. explicit failure/reintegration rather than streak punishment.

Clove does **not** initially provide:

- unmoderated community chat;
- anonymous DMs;
- one-to-one mentor matching;
- diagnosis or treatment;
- “masculinity scores”;
- public rank leaderboards;
- proof-by-popularity;
- punishment for a missed mission;
- claims that service/sport/trades are treatments for loneliness;
- minors.

# 14. Measurement hypothesis

The first scientific/product claim should remain modest:

> A Clove mission flow can get some 18–24-year-old male users to complete a self-selected, externally checkable real-world action and return to reflect on it without requiring an account, addictive engagement mechanics, or simulated social approval.

Potential primary outcome:

`mission_completed / mission_started`

Secondary outcomes:

- time from mission selection to first physical action;
- returned for debrief;
- self-rated usefulness/competence before vs after;
- whether another person actually benefited;
- voluntary second mission start;
- failure + reintegration rate;
- adverse-event / “this made things worse” feedback.

Belonging, purpose and loneliness should be secondary exploratory outcomes in the first pilot, not proof claims.

# 15. F0 verdict

**BROTHERHOOD EVIDENCE LEDGER: PASS_WITH_DISCLOSED_LIMITS**

The central design intuition survives, but in a narrower form:

- belonging and social connection matter;
- young adulthood is a genuine low-belonging period in Canadian data;
- mentoring can help modestly, but requires quality and safe relationship management;
- competence-producing paths such as apprenticeship have strong real-world labor value but are not established loneliness treatments;
- service/volunteering is associated with flourishing but causal effects are not established;
- purpose can be cultivated but no single recipe dominates;
- sport/martial-arts psychosocial claims are more mixed than the initial report suggested;
- rites-of-passage evidence is promising but early;
- peer/group interventions are not automatically beneficial;
- no current evidence licenses the full “Brotherhood Without an Enemy” package as an established intervention.

Therefore the project is authorized to proceed only as a **measured behavioral product experiment**, not as a therapeutic or social-science claim of solved male alienation.

## Largest remaining evidence gap

There is no direct evidence yet that the proposed combination

`valued direction → real-world mission → observable competence/usefulness → debrief → increasing responsibility → eventual mentorship`

causes improved belonging or purpose in men aged 18–24.

That is the claim Clove must earn rather than assume.
