TITLE: WHERE DID THE BOYS GO?
SUBTITLE: Before we pick a villain, can we find out when the gaps actually appeared?
SCHEDULE: August 25, 2026

Everybody already has an answer.

Feminism.

Porn.

Phones.

Universities.

Fathers.

Capitalism.

Video games.

Toxic masculinity.

Not enough masculinity.

Fantastic.

Before we hold the trial, can we find the fucking body?

Because something real has happened to boys and young men in several parts of modern life.

But “several parts” matters.

Education is not employment.

Employment is not suicide.

Suicide is not dating.

Dating is not fatherhood.

Fatherhood is not incarceration.

If those trends began at different times, then one giant explanation probably does not explain all of them.

That is where I want to start.

Not with a villain.

With a timeline.

Take university.

In the United States, women had already moved ahead of men in total college enrollment by around 1980. This is not something that suddenly appeared after TikTok, Andrew Tate, DEI, COVID, Tinder or whatever happened last Tuesday.

And the gap is still visible.

Among Americans who graduated high school in 2024, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that 69.5 percent of young women enrolled in college compared with 55.4 percent of young men.

That is a large gap.

It is also not proof that anybody “purged” men.

A gap tells us what happened.

It does not automatically tell us why.

Maybe schooling rewards some skills more than others.

Maybe boys mature differently on average in ways that interact badly with the school environment.

Maybe labour-market options change the value of university differently for men and women.

Maybe family structure matters.

Maybe boys receive different expectations.

Maybe institutions corrected real disadvantages facing girls and women and then became slower to recognize emerging disadvantages facing boys and men.

Maybe some of those are right.

Maybe all of them explain a small piece.

Maybe my favourite theory gets murdered by the dates.

Good.

That is the point.

Canada is now openly acknowledging that men and boys have distinct problems across health, education, social connection and other parts of life. Health Canada says men have shorter life expectancy, higher suicide and premature mortality, lower engagement with primary and preventive care, and lower education levels.

Again: that does not give us one cause.

It gives us a fucking investigation.

This is where internet arguments usually go bad.

Somebody sees the outcome first.

Then they pick a moral story.

If the person already dislikes feminism, feminism caused it.

If the person already dislikes masculinity, masculinity caused it.

If the person already dislikes capitalism, capitalism caused it.

If the person already dislikes video games, congratulations, apparently PlayStation killed civilization.

Maybe one of those things matters.

But the timeline gets veto power.

If the trend began before your culprit existed, your culprit did not start the trend.

It might have accelerated it.

It might have changed its shape.

It might have nothing to do with it.

Those are different claims.

So “Where Did the Boys Go?” is not going to begin as a book about how men were victimized.

It is going to begin as a missing-person report.

Where did boys start disappearing from university?

When did male labour-force participation begin changing?

When did marriage and fatherhood change?

When did social isolation change?

What happened to suicide, overdose, incarceration and homelessness?

Which trends are uniquely male?

Which are class effects wearing a male face?

Which differ by race, geography or education?

Which are actually getting better while the internet keeps telling us everything is getting worse?

And this part matters most:

What evidence would prove the popular story wrong?

Because “young men are struggling” can become just as self-sealing as any other ideology.

If every unemployed man proves the theory, every lonely man proves it, every angry man proves it, every successful man is dismissed as an exception and every contrary trend is ignored, then we have not built a model.

We built a religion with graphs.

I do not want that.

I want dates.

Denominators.

Competing explanations.

And the humiliating possibility that some things I have been saying are wrong.

The angry version of this conversation says:

They got rid of the men.

The dismissive version says:

Men had every advantage. If they are struggling now, that's on them.

I think both are too fucking easy.

Something happened.

Probably several somethings.

Now we find out what.

BORING RECEIPT

U.S. National Center for Education Statistics historical enrollment tables show women had already surpassed men in total degree-granting postsecondary enrollment by 1980. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that among 2024 recent high-school graduates ages 16–24, 69.5% of women and 55.4% of men were enrolled in college in October 2024.

Health Canada’s 2026 Men and Boys’ Health Strategy materials state that men in Canada face distinct challenges including shorter life expectancy, higher suicide and premature mortality, lower engagement with primary/preventive care and lower education levels. These observations do not establish a single cause.

TITLE: THE MARKET FOUND LOOKSMAXXING
SUBTITLE: A fringe language of male inadequacy just learned how to sell moisturizer and hamburgers.
SCHEDULE: August 28, 2026

There is a beautiful little life cycle on the internet.

Step one: a weird subculture invents a word.

Step two: normal people discover it.

Step three: teenagers turn it into a joke.

Step four: a marketing department discovers the joke six months late.

Step five: your hotel starts mogging you.

We have apparently reached step five.

On August 21, The Wall Street Journal reported that brands including CeraVe, Carl's Jr. and Marriott were using “maxxing” and “mogging” language in marketing.

That matters to me because I have been digging into looksmaxxing as part of a bigger question about young men, loneliness, appearance and the market for male inadequacy.

And now the language has crossed a line.

Not necessarily a dangerous line.

A commercial one.

Words that grew inside fringe appearance-obsessed and incel-adjacent spaces are now ordinary enough that somebody in a conference room can use them to sell you skincare without needing a twenty-minute explanation and a hazmat suit.

That is cultural mainstreaming.

It is not proof of harm.

Important difference.

If CeraVe says “maxxing,” that does not magically cause body dysmorphia.

If somebody says “sleepmaxxing,” I am not going to tackle him into a de-radicalization van.

The suffix has become generic.

Gymmaxxing.

Sleepmaxxing.

Moneymaxxing.

Skincaremaxxing.

Probably taxreturnmaxxing by April.

The Institute for Strategic Dialogue published something useful the day before the Wall Street Journal article.

Their August 20 piece was about what they call “lexical laundering”: fringe vocabulary escaping its original habitat and becoming normal enough that most people using it no longer know where it came from.

Their example of “-maxxing” is especially useful because it complicates the story.

Looksmaxxing did grow from spaces where appearance could be treated as destiny: wrong jaw, wrong height, wrong skull, wrong genes, game over.

But once the language spreads, the origin does not necessarily travel with it.

A kid saying “gymmaxxing” may mean exactly what your grandfather meant by “go lift some fucking weights.”

That is why I do not want to make the mistake of treating vocabulary as diagnosis.

The more interesting question is what happens to the underlying idea.

There is a version of self-improvement that says:

I want to look better.

Fine.

There is another version that says:

My appearance determines my human worth, romantic future and social rank, and I must continuously optimize the product because the product is me.

That is different.

Now add a market.

Markets are very good at taking an insecurity, cleaning the blood off the edges and putting it in attractive packaging.

You are inadequate.

Luckily, inadequacy is available in six easy payments.

This is hardly unique to men.

Women have been sold versions of this bullshit forever.

Too fat.

Too old.

Wrong skin.

Wrong hair.

Wrong ass.

Here is the cream.

Here is the surgery.

Here is the diet.

Here is the thing standing between current you and lovable you.

Maybe what is new is not that capitalism discovered insecurity.

That would be the least surprising discovery since a Labrador found a sandwich.

Maybe what is new is the male version becoming culturally legible enough to commercialize openly.

And there is another piece worth watching.

A recent commentary in Feminist Media Studies argues that in some looksmaxxing/manospheric spaces, pain from extreme body modification can be interpreted as evidence of appearance-based injustice.

That gives us a possible pathway:

I hate how I look.

I hurt myself trying to fix it.

The pain proves the system is unfair.

The unfairness needs someone to blame.

Now private suffering has a political story attached to it.

That pathway is plausible.

It is not established as common.

We do not know how many boys travel it.

And I want to keep that uncertainty visible because this subject is begging for a moral panic.

The internet is already full of people who see one teenager mewing and immediately imagine a convoy heading toward fascism.

Calm the fuck down.

A boy wanting a stronger jaw is not a political ideology.

A boy believing his jaw determines whether his life is worth living is a different problem.

That is where I think the useful intervention lives.

Not:

Stop caring how you look.

That is stupid. Humans care how they look.

Not:

Looks don't matter.

Also stupid. Appearance affects human judgment.

The better message is:

Your appearance is information.

It is not your fucking constitution.

Improve what you want.

Lift weights.

Dress better.

Fix your teeth.

Use moisturizer.

Sleep.

But the second somebody convinces you that human worth can be reduced to a facial score and continuously optimized like a stock portfolio, inspect what they are selling next.

Because apparently Marriott has entered the chat.

BORING RECEIPT

It is a conceptual/qualitative contribution, not population evidence establishing prevalence or causation.

TITLE: EQUAL DOES NOT MEAN IDENTICAL
SUBTITLE: Fair is not a photocopier.
SCHEDULE: September 1, 2026

I have a stupid little picture in my head whenever somebody says equality means giving everyone exactly the same thing.

A mechanic walks into a shop carrying one wrench.

Transmission?

Same wrench.

Spark plug?

Same wrench.

Windshield?

Same wrench.

Broken radio?

Same fucking wrench.

Then he stands back proudly and says:

Equality.

That is not equality.

That is a mechanic who owns one wrench.

We already understand this in medicine whenever the difference is impossible to ignore.

Pregnancy is not a male condition.

Prostate cancer is not a female condition.

Bodies differ.

Risks differ.

Life stages differ.

The hard part begins when the difference is not that obvious.

Mental health.

Help-seeking.

Substance use.

Social isolation.

Preventive care.

The environment people respond to.

The language that makes someone stay or leave.

Canada is currently building its first Men and Boys' Health Strategy because the government itself now says men and boys have distinct health challenges.

Men live shorter lives on average.

They die by suicide at higher rates.

They represent most opioid-related deaths.

They use some preventive and primary-care services less.

Those are outcomes.

They are not a diagnosis of the cause.

That distinction is where I want to plant a flag.

Because the stupidest possible response to different outcomes is to immediately declare one explanation morally correct.

Men die by suicide more because masculinity is toxic.

Maybe some masculine norms matter.

Men do not seek care because doctors do not understand men.

Maybe service design matters too.

Biology.

Work.

Risk exposure.

Income.

Culture.

Relationships.

Addiction.

Trauma.

Access.

Communication.

All potentially relevant.

The fact that one explanation is politically convenient does not make the others disappear.

That is why “equal does not mean identical” is not an answer.

It is permission to ask better questions.

Would some men engage better with care framed around practical problem-solving?

Would some prefer group activity to sitting face-to-face in a room?

Would same-sex clinician choice matter in sensitive settings?

Would peer support help some groups?

Would it hurt others?

Does a male-responsive approach improve outcomes or merely feel more comfortable?

Measure it.

And apply the same rule to women.

If women respond differently to a treatment, environment or communication style, study it.

If men do, study that too.

This is where equality arguments get strangely frightened of reality.

As if finding a sex difference threatens human dignity.

Why?

A difference in average height does not make one sex morally superior.

A difference in disease risk does not assign voting rights.

A difference in treatment preference does not cancel equal citizenship.

Equal worth is a moral rule.

Identical bodies is an empirical claim.

They are not the same sentence.

And there is another danger on my side of this argument.

Once you start talking about sex differences, people can get drunk on categories.

Men are like this.

Women are like that.

No.

Group averages do not get the final vote on a person.

If research says men on average prefer X and the man in front of you prefers Y, congratulations, you have met an individual human being.

Update the model.

That is the symmetry I want.

Study the group.

Meet the person.

Let the person overrule the category.

Because the goal is not male care.

Or female care.

The goal is care that works.

Sometimes that may mean the same thing for everybody.

Sometimes it may not.

The test is not whether the treatment looks equal from across the parking lot.

The test is whether people receive an equal chance at effective benefit.

Same door.

Different result.

Forty years.

At some point somebody should inspect the fucking door.

BORING RECEIPT

Health Canada states that men in Canada face distinct health challenges including shorter life expectancy, higher premature death and suicide, high representation in opioid-related deaths and lower engagement with primary/preventive care. Canada's first Men and Boys' Health Strategy is scheduled for fall 2026. Health Canada also explicitly states that a focus on men's health does not replace work supporting women's health.

These outcome differences do not show that men universally require different treatment, nor do they identify one cause. The argument in this essay is a service-design principle: where meaningful differences are plausible, measure them rather than assume sameness or difference.

TITLE: DO MEN SOMETIMES NEED MEN?
SUBTITLE: If the person across the desk matters, why are we afraid to measure it?
SCHEDULE: September 4, 2026

Here is a question that should be incredibly boring.

Does the sex of your doctor or therapist ever matter?

Not always.

Not morally.

Not because men possess a mystical penis-based Wi-Fi connection that lets us communicate through beard vibrations.

Just:

Does it ever affect trust, disclosure, comfort, staying in treatment or outcomes?

That should be measurable.

And in 2026, the evidence says something wonderfully inconvenient.

Sometimes.

Maybe.

Depends.

Which is science's favourite way of ruining a good internet argument.

A systematic review published this year looked at 27 studies on doctor-patient gender concordance and communication, trust, satisfaction and preferences.

Overall, same-gender matching was often associated with better communication and trust, especially for women.

But satisfaction results were mixed.

Some studies found benefits.

Some found no meaningful association.

Some combinations were even worse.

Then a separate 2026 scoping review looked specifically at mental health.

Its conclusion was even more useful for what I care about:

Most evidence was neutral or mixed.

But male patients with substance-use disorders may benefit from gender matching in some settings.

That is not:

MEN NEED MALE THERAPISTS.

That is:

We found a place where the variable might matter. Keep looking.

Which is exactly the point.

I have heard people talk about therapist matching as though considering sex is either obviously necessary or obviously sexist.

Why are those the only choices?

If a woman who experienced sexual violence says she would be more comfortable with a female clinician, most reasonable people can understand that without holding a congressional hearing about equality.

If a man says there are things he would find easier to tell another man, why would the scientific response be to decide in advance that this preference is either enlightened or pathological?

Ask him.

Then measure whether it changes anything.

Comfort is not the same as outcome.

Preference is not the same as effectiveness.

But neither is irrelevant.

A patient who never returns to the room cannot benefit from the theoretically perfect therapy waiting inside it.

That is why I keep coming back to the front door.

We tell men:

Ask for help.

Fine.

When he does, what makes him stay?

Maybe provider sex matters for almost nobody.

Maybe it matters mainly in sensitive conditions.

Maybe communication style matters far more.

The general physician literature actually points in that direction too: empathy, clear information, active listening and shared decision-making are associated with trust.

Maybe a great female clinician is a better fit for a particular man than a mediocre male clinician.

Obviously.

The person gets the final vote.

So here is the policy I would like to see tested:

Meaningful choice where feasible.

Not segregation.

Not men must treat men.

Not women must treat women.

Choice.

Then track what happens.

Who chooses same-sex care?

For what conditions?

Does it change disclosure?

Does it change therapeutic alliance?

Return rates?

Dropout?

Symptoms?

Recovery?

Do the effects disappear once communication style is controlled?

Now we are doing something useful.

I do not want a men's movement that replaces one untested stereotype with another.

“Men do better with men” is exactly the kind of sentence that sounds true if you already believe it.

So make it earn the right to survive.

The evidence so far is mixed.

That is not disappointing.

That is where the fucking research starts.

BORING RECEIPT

Esposito et al. published a 2026 systematic review of 27 studies on doctor-patient gender concordance. It found generally better communication/trust in some concordant dyads, particularly for women, but mixed satisfaction results and null or negative findings in some contexts.

Lanni et al. published a July 2026 scoping review of gender concordance in mental-health care. Its conclusion was that effects are context dependent, with mostly neutral or mixed findings overall; male patients with substance-use disorders were one area where gender matching may provide benefit.

Neither review supports the blanket claim that men need male clinicians.

TITLE: THE LIBRARY OF ALEXANDRIA IS 24 INCHES FROM YOUR NUTS
SUBTITLE: We gave humanity almost all the world's knowledge and forgot to teach it how to think.
SCHEDULE: September 8, 2026

There is a decent chance you are reading this while sitting on a toilet.

Which means a device approximately two feet from your reproductive equipment can access more information than most kings, generals, professors and entire civilizations could have imagined.

And we're using it to argue with a stranger named CumLord927 about whether a celebrity's jawline is natural.

Humanity is magnificent.

I am not anti-phone.

I love the fucking thing.

Maps.

Weather.

Banking.

Medical information.

Books.

Research papers.

Translation.

A camera.

A calculator.

Every song I grew up with.

And now an artificial researcher that can explain quantum mechanics, rewrite a paragraph and help me find a government report before my coffee gets cold.

This is not a sermon about throwing technology into the sea.

It is a question about training.

In the United States, 91 percent of adults now own a smartphone. Among adults under 50, it is about 97 percent.

We did not merely invent a popular gadget.

We installed a permanent portal to the information environment in almost everyone's pocket.

Then our stewardship curriculum was approximately:

Don't lose the charger.

Imagine doing that with anything else this powerful.

We make somebody learn road rules before driving a car.

We teach apprentices not to stick a screwdriver into the exciting part of an electrical panel.

We train soldiers before handing them weapons.

Then we give a thirteen-year-old a machine connected to pornography, gambling mechanics, political propaganda, strangers, status competition, advertising, infinite entertainment, algorithmic ranking and now AI.

Good luck, champ.

Remember your password.

The problem is not access to information.

The problem is confusing access with understanding.

GPS can get me somewhere without teaching me the map.

A calculator can produce the answer without teaching me arithmetic.

Search can retrieve a fact without teaching me how to evaluate a source.

AI can produce an argument without teaching me whether the argument is true.

That does not make the tools bad.

It means the tool can perform the task without necessarily training the person.

That distinction is going to matter more and more.

Because convenience removes friction.

Usually that is wonderful.

I do not want to return to a world where looking up one obscure fact requires driving forty minutes to a library and interrogating a microfiche machine designed by Satan.

Some friction deserved to die.

But some friction was practice.

Remembering a route exercised spatial memory.

Writing the paragraph exercised composition.

Doing the calculation exercised number sense.

Waiting before responding gave your temper time to stop running the government.

If we remove the task, we should at least ask whether we also removed the training.

That is digital temperance to me.

Not abstinence.

Stewardship.

Know what the machine is doing for you.

Know what skill you are choosing not to practice.

Sometimes offload it happily.

I do not need to manually calculate my taxes with an abacus to prove I am a man.

But keep the things that build judgment.

If the machine gives you an answer, ask where the answer came from.

If search gives you the first result, remember that “first” is a ranking position, not an epistemological medal.

If AI writes your argument, make sure you understand the argument well enough to defend it without the machine sitting on your lap like a courtroom ventriloquist.

And above all, do not trust me because I sound confident.

I use the same machine.

Check the receipt.

That might be the single digital skill worth teaching before all the others:

Reality gets the last fucking vote.

BORING RECEIPT

Pew Research Center's 2025 survey found that 91% of U.S. adults owned a smartphone, including 97% of adults under 50. This essay uses GPS, calculators, search and AI as examples of cognitive offloading. The claim that offloading necessarily causes broad cognitive decline is not established here. The narrower argument is that task performance and skill development are not identical things and should be evaluated separately.

TITLE: THE VANISHING PAUSE
SUBTITLE: Civilization used to have a few seconds between stupid thought and permanent consequence.
SCHEDULE: September 11, 2026

A lot of civilization is just shit humans invented to stop the first impulse from becoming the final action.

Doors.

Queues.

Court procedures.

Waiting periods.

Editors.

The phrase “sleep on it.”

Your mother saying, “Do not send that fucking message.”

We built little speed bumps between feeling and doing.

Then we invented the smartphone and gave every human impulse a Send button.

Angry?

Send.

Horny?

Send.

Jealous?

Post.

Lonely?

Buy.

Scared?

Search until you find an explanation that makes the fear feel intelligent.

Offended?

Summon the village before your heart rate comes down.

Now add AI.

The old sequence might have been:

Impulse.

Think.

Cool off.

Try to explain yourself.

Realize halfway through the explanation that maybe you are being a dick.

New sequence:

Impulse.

Ask machine to explain why you are right.

Receive polished argument.

Post.

Human judgment has been upgraded to Amazon Prime.

Same-day delivery.

That is funny until you realize the delay was doing something.

Not always.

Sometimes delay was just inconvenience.

Waiting six weeks for a bank form did not build character. It built hatred of banks.

But emotional delay is different.

There is a reason people tell you not to send the email while angry.

The first version of your thought is often written by whatever chemical has temporarily occupied the control room.

Rage writes one draft.

Fear writes another.

Lust has never read the terms and conditions.

The pause gives the rest of you time to arrive.

This is why my digital-temperance argument is not “phones made everyone stupid.”

Humans were doing spectacularly stupid things long before Wi-Fi.

The phone did something more interesting.

It lowered the cost and time required to convert an impulse into an external event.

One angry sentence can now become:

A screenshot.

A workplace complaint.

A viral clip.

A relationship ending.

A permanent search result.

A mob.

Sometimes the consequences are deserved.

Sometimes they are ridiculous.

The important change is speed.

Judgment is now racing machinery designed to remove delay.

And machinery is faster.

So maybe the modern skill is not just critical thinking.

Maybe it is deliberate friction.

Put a speed bump back where the machine removed one.

Draft the message and do not send it.

Leave the phone outside the bedroom.

Wait ten minutes before buying the thing.

Read the article before sharing the headline.

Ask AI for the strongest argument against your own position before asking it to make your position sound brilliant.

Turn off the notification that keeps recruiting your nervous system into somebody else's business model.

None of these are sacred rituals.

They are experiments.

The point is to recover one tiny human power:

I can feel something without immediately obeying it.

That is temperance.

The old temperance movement looked at alcohol and said:

This substance can get its hands on human judgment.

The new version should be broader.

What has its hands on you?

Notifications?

Porn?

News?

Shopping?

Political outrage?

Validation?

AI agreeing with you?

The answer does not have to be “delete everything.”

Sometimes the answer is simply:

I noticed the hand.

Now I decide whether I want it there.

Civilization is partly the machinery humans build between impulse and action.

Maybe digital stewardship begins by rebuilding a few of those missing seconds.

BORING RECEIPT

Smartphones are now near-ubiquitous in many populations; Pew reported 91% smartphone ownership among U.S. adults in its 2025 survey. The broader “vanishing pause” argument in this essay is a conceptual hypothesis about reduced friction between impulse and action. It should not be read as a claim that smartphones uniquely cause impulsivity, aggression or poor judgment.
