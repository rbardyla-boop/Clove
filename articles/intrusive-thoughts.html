Cognitive Science for Operators

Intrusive Thoughts Are Not What You Think They Are

By Ryan Bardyla · Canadian Army Veteran · From Stubborn Bastard

The Thought That Showed Up Uninvited

It appeared without warning. You were driving, or standing in a queue, or trying to fall asleep, and your brain produced something — an image, a sentence, a scenario — that you would never choose to think. Something violent, sexual, taboo, catastrophic, or simply so contrary to everything you believe about yourself that its presence felt like an accusation.

And then, because the thought was disturbing, you did what felt logical: you tried to push it away. You told yourself not to think it. You catalogued it as evidence of something wrong with you. You monitored your own mind for its return, the way you'd monitor a room after hearing something move in the walls. And it came back. Louder, more persistent, more frequent — as if the act of fighting it had given it power.

It had. That's the mechanism. And understanding it changes everything about how you respond to what your brain generates.

What the Brain Actually Does

The brain is a prediction engine, not a values statement. Its job is to model possibilities — all of them, including dangerous ones — so that threats can be anticipated and responses prepared. A brain that only generated thoughts consistent with your values would be a useless brain. It would have no model of what could go wrong, no capacity to anticipate danger, no ability to plan for adverse scenarios.

So the brain generates everything. It runs simulations of scenarios you'd never choose and outcomes you'd never want, not because it endorses them but because modeling them is part of how it keeps you safe. Most people have these thoughts and move on, because the thought is recognized as a background process — generated content, not a message. The thought passes without attaching.

The people who suffer from intrusive thoughts are not generating different thoughts. They are relating differently to the same background noise that everyone's brain produces. Specifically: they are treating the thought as meaningful. As a signal about who they are, what they're capable of, what they secretly want. That interpretation triggers anxiety. The anxiety triggers monitoring. The monitoring amplifies the signal. The thought returns with more force because you've flagged it as important, and the brain prioritizes important signals.

The thought is not the problem. The relationship with the thought is the problem.

"Having a thought about something doesn't mean you want it, believe it, or are capable of it. The brain generates content the same way a search engine returns results — the results are not endorsements. They're a database query."

The Suppression Paradox

There is a famous experiment: don't think about a white bear. Try not to think about a white bear for the next sixty seconds. What happens? The white bear is everywhere. The instruction to suppress the thought dramatically increases its frequency.

This is not a quirk of the white bear experiment. It is how the brain processes suppression instructions generally. To suppress a thought, the brain must first represent the thought — must hold it in working memory as a reference for what to avoid — and then check repeatedly that it's not present. The checking mechanism is itself the generator. The monitoring produces what it monitors for.

Every attempt to not have the intrusive thought is an instruction to your brain to keep the thought highly active in working memory. Every "why did I just think that?" is a flag that tells the prediction system: this content is important, prioritize it. Suppression is gasoline. The response that actually works is the opposite of suppression, and it is deeply counterintuitive.

The Defusion Protocol

TOOL 01
Defusion — The Language Shift
From ACT (Acceptance and Commitment Therapy): replace "I think X" with "I notice I'm having the thought that X." This is not wordplay. The first formulation makes the thought identity — you are fused with it. The second creates an observer: there is a you, and there is a thought the you is observing. The observer is not the thought. This gap — the space between the you who notices and the content being noticed — is where the thought loses its authority. It becomes what it actually is: a generated output from a prediction system, not a confession or a verdict.
TOOL 02
Allowing Without Engaging
The instruction is not "make the thought go away." The instruction is "let it be there without doing anything about it." Sit with the thought present. Don't fight it. Don't analyze it. Don't try to understand why you had it or what it means about you. Allow it the same way you allow traffic noise — it's there, it's not a signal requiring action, it will pass when it passes. The anxiety that attaches to intrusive thoughts is not produced by the thought itself — it's produced by the struggle against it. Stop struggling. The thought, un-fought, has a half-life. It will diminish without your help.
TOOL 03
The Values Anchor
The reason intrusive thoughts are so disturbing is often that they seem to contradict something you deeply value. A violent thought contradicts your care for the people in your life. A thought about betrayal contradicts your commitment to integrity. That contradiction — the gap between the content and your actual values — is the distress signal. Run it forward: the presence of the distress is actually evidence of the value, not evidence that the thought is true. You are not distressed by thoughts about harming people you don't care about. The distress exists because you care. The thought and the distress are opposite signals pointing to the same value.
TOOL 04
Exposure Without Compulsion
If intrusive thoughts are running on the suppression loop — fight → return → fight harder → return louder — the exit is deliberate exposure without the compulsive response. That means: when the thought appears, notice it, label it as a brain-generated output, allow it to be present, and refrain from the checking, reassurance-seeking, or suppression behavior that has been feeding the loop. Each time you do this, you provide the prediction system with new data: this content does not require emergency response. The system updates. It takes repetition — each pass through the loop without the compulsion response weakens the loop's hold incrementally.

What the Thought Doesn't Mean

The thought does not mean you are dangerous. It does not mean you secretly want what the thought depicted. It does not mean you are broken, evil, or fundamentally different from other people. Research consistently shows that the content of intrusive thoughts is similar across populations — people who have never struggled with them have the same kinds of thoughts and simply don't attach significance to them.

The thought is noise from a pattern-completion system that runs 24 hours a day and generates content across the full spectrum of human experience, including its most disturbing edges. Its presence means the system is running. That's all it means.

What you do next is the only part that matters. The protocol is not to have different thoughts. It is to have a different relationship with the ones that arrive uninvited.
