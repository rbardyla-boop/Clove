Psychology for Operators

Imposter Syndrome: Why Success Makes It Worse, Not Better

By Ryan Bardyla · Canadian Army Veteran · From Stubborn Bastard

The Evidence That Doesn't Land

You have the job. The credential. The track record. People treat you as if you know what you're doing — and you've noticed, with a specific kind of unease, that the gap between how they see you and how you see yourself is not closing. If anything, it's widening. The more external evidence accumulates, the more certain you are that the evidence is wrong, that the people trusting you are operating on incomplete information, and that at some point the gap will become visible and the whole construction will collapse.

The standard advice is to accumulate more evidence. To remember your wins. To build confidence through success. The problem is that you've been doing that — and the feeling has not followed. You have the wins. The feeling remains. Something is wrong with the model that says confidence is a byproduct of evidence, because the evidence is in and the confidence is not.

The Calibration Error

Imposter syndrome is not low self-esteem in the general sense. People with low self-esteem don't usually achieve at a level that puts them in environments where imposter syndrome can develop. It is a specific calibration error: the internal evidence base does not match the external record.

Here's why: you have complete access to your own incompetence, uncertainty, and behind-the-scenes struggle. You know every decision you agonized over, every moment of not knowing, every time you were figuring it out as you went and hoping no one noticed. You have the full unedited feed. What you see of others is the edited version — what they chose to present, the competence they projected, the appearance of certainty they maintained while having exactly the same internal experience you're having.

You are comparing your unedited interior to everyone else's highlight reel. The comparison produces a result that confirms the imposter narrative: they know what they're doing, and I am figuring it out. That comparison is the calibration error. Everyone is figuring it out. You are simply more aware of your own process than of theirs.

The second mechanism is attribution. High performers with imposter syndrome consistently attribute their successes to external factors — luck, timing, other people's work, circumstances — while attributing failures to internal ones. The success doesn't count; you got lucky. The failure counts; you were insufficient. This attribution pattern means no amount of external success updates the internal model, because the internal model has a pre-installed filter that excludes success from the evidence base.

"You have full access to your own uncertainty and zero access to everyone else's. Of course you feel like the only person who doesn't fully know what they're doing. You're the only person whose internal experience you can actually read."

Why It Gets Worse at Higher Levels

Competent people tend to move into roles where the competence gap is real — where they genuinely don't yet have all the skills required, because they've been promoted into territory that requires growth. This is the correct trajectory. It means you are in the right places. But it also means that at each level, the gap between "what I know" and "what this role requires" is real, and the imposter narrative has real material to work with.

Lower-level roles produce lower-level imposter syndrome because the competence gap is smaller. As the role demands more, the gap is larger, and the feeling intensifies. The irony is that imposter syndrome often signals that you are exactly where growth happens — in a role that is appropriately challenging — and the feeling is being interpreted as evidence that you don't belong, when it is actually evidence that you do.

The Calibration Protocol

TOOL 01
The Evidence Audit — Both Columns
The imposter narrative runs on a one-sided evidence base: the column of uncertainty, missed marks, and struggles. The counter-audit forces the other column: specific outcomes you produced, specific decisions that were correct, specific times you delivered under conditions that required real competence. Not feelings of competence — outcomes. The brain is running a mental filter (see: cognitive distortions) that excludes positive data. The explicit audit forces both columns into view at the same time. The narrative doesn't disappear, but it loses the claim to completeness that makes it feel like certainty.
TOOL 02
The Attribution Rebalance
For your last three significant successes: list the factors that contributed. For each factor you've labeled as luck or circumstance, run the test — would a different person in the same circumstance have produced the same outcome? Usually no. The circumstance was an input. Your decisions, preparation, and execution were also inputs. You don't get to own the failure unconditionally and disclaim the success entirely. The attribution model is applying different rules to different outcomes. The rebalance applies consistent rules to both.
TOOL 03
The Internal Access Problem
Select one person in your environment who seems to operate with the confidence you lack. Ask yourself: what is their internal experience when they walk into a room they don't fully understand? You don't know. You have access to their external presentation only. Now apply the same logic to yourself: the people in your environment have access to your external presentation only. They are not seeing the internal uncertainty. They are seeing what you project — which is, evidently, sufficient to be read as competent. The calibration problem runs in one direction only. You have disproportionate access to your own uncertainty. That asymmetry is the entire source of the gap.
TOOL 04
The After-Action Protocol
After each significant performance — a presentation, a difficult decision, a high-stakes conversation — run a structured debrief: what worked, what didn't, and what the evidence says about your capacity in this domain. The imposter narrative runs on memory edited by the mental filter. The formal debrief produces a written record that the filter cannot retroactively edit. Over time, the written record is more accurate than the feeling-based memory, and the record accumulates into a document of evidence the attribution model cannot exclude. You are building an internal file the imposter narrative has to compete against.

The Competence Paradox

Research on expertise consistently finds that novices overestimate their competence (they don't know what they don't know), while experts underestimate it (they know exactly how much they don't know). Imposter syndrome clusters at the expert end of that distribution. The feeling of not knowing enough is a signature of knowing enough to understand the scope of what there is to know.

The people who never feel like imposters are often the people who should. The people carrying the weight of "I'm not sure I belong here" are often the people whose self-assessment is the most rigorous — and whose bar for their own performance is the highest.

That doesn't make the feeling go away. But it should change how you interpret it. The feeling is not evidence of fraudulence. It is evidence of a self-assessment system that takes the work seriously. That system is an asset. It just needs the complete evidence set to run on, not the filtered version it's been using.
