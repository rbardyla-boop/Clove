Trauma-Informed Neuroscience

What Hypervigilance Actually Is (And Why You Can't Just Relax)

By Ryan Bardyla · Canadian Army Veteran · From The Biological Bruise

What They Don't Understand

People keep telling you to relax. You're too intense. You read too much into things. Stop looking for problems that aren't there. Calm down. Why are you always on edge?

Here's the thing: they're not wrong about what they're observing. You are scanning constantly. You are reading the room every time you walk into one. You are monitoring tone of voice, tracking exits, cataloguing small behavioral shifts in the people around you before most people have even noticed them. You are, functionally, always partially on duty.

What they don't understand — and what you may not have had words for — is that this is not a choice. It is not a personality trait that can be adjusted by deciding to be different. It is a nervous system that was retrained by experience, running the protocol that experience taught it. You cannot "just relax" a system that was recalibrated at a hardware level by sustained exposure to unpredictable threat.

The Recalibration Mechanism

Your nervous system is not a fixed object. It is plastic — it rewires based on the environment it operates in. This is the brain's greatest asset in normal circumstances: it adapts. The adaptation that produces hypervigilance is the same plasticity that makes humans capable of learning anything. It just learned the wrong thing for the current environment.

After sustained exposure to threat — chronic stress, volatile home environments, combat, abuse, or any situation where danger was unpredictable and missing a signal was costly — the amygdala raises its sensitivity threshold. The threat-detection system recalibrates: things that wouldn't previously trigger a response now do, because the training data said that the cost of a miss is higher than the cost of a false positive. Better to scan for ten things that aren't threats than to miss one that is.

The scanning behavior — monitoring faces for microexpressions, cataloguing tone shifts, tracking who is positioned where, anticipating hostility before it materializes — is not paranoia. It is a threat-detection system that was trained on a higher-density threat environment running its learned protocol in a lower-density one. The system is not malfunctioning. It is functioning exactly as designed based on the inputs it received.

The problem is that the brain does not automatically recalibrate downward when the threat environment changes. The amygdala does not receive a memo that the war is over. It continues running the old protocol indefinitely, until something teaches it otherwise. Without deliberate intervention, a nervous system trained to threat-scan in one environment will run that scan in every environment, forever.

"Hypervigilance is the correct program running in the wrong environment. The system is not broken. The threat map it's using is outdated. That's a different problem with a different solution."

What It Feels Like From the Inside

Exhaustion that doesn't resolve with rest, because the system never fully goes offline. Tight shoulders and jaw tension that you carry as a baseline. Difficulty in crowds or unfamiliar social environments — too many inputs to track, the scan overwhelms. Startling easily. Difficulty sleeping because the watchpost doesn't stand down at night.

Hyperreactivity to tone. A slightly sharp word from someone feels like an attack because the system is calibrated for an environment where a slightly sharp tone was a leading indicator of danger. Irritability that seems disproportionate to the trigger, because the trigger activated a protocol built for a much higher-stakes version of the situation.

And underneath all of it, the exhaustion of never being fully off. Never arriving somewhere and actually arriving — some part of you is always still running the assessment. It is genuinely tiring to run a threat-detection system at high sensitivity all day. The people who tell you to relax have never had to carry that operational load.

The Recalibration Protocol

TOOL 01
Name the Program
In the moment the scan activates — when you feel the hypervigilant response run — name what's happening. Not "I'm anxious" or "I'm overreacting." Those frame it as character deficits. The accurate frame is: "My threat-detection system has flagged an input. I'm going to assess whether the flag is accurate." This is not minimizing. It's accurate. You're running an assessment on the assessment's output. That small linguistic shift creates enough distance to choose a response rather than just executing the protocol automatically.
TOOL 02
The Anchor Protocol
A physiological interrupt for when the system is running hot: feet flat on the floor. Five breaths, exhale longer than inhale (4 count in, 7 hold, 8 out). Name five things you can physically see in the immediate environment. This is not meditation — it is a forced redirect of sensory processing to present-tense data. The hypervigilant system runs on past threat patterns and future threat projection. The anchor pulls attention to current sensory input, where the actual threat level of the present moment can be assessed independently of the historical data.
TOOL 03
The Daily Threat Audit
At the end of the day: log how many threat-flags the scan generated. How many were accurate? This is not a judgment exercise — it's a calibration data-collection exercise. Over time, you will see your system's false-positive rate. That rate is concrete information. It tells you the gap between your calibrated sensitivity and the actual threat density of your current environment. When the nervous system has enough accumulated evidence that the current environment is genuinely lower-threat than the one it was calibrated on, recalibration becomes possible.
TOOL 04
Safe Context Accumulation
Identify three environments where the scan goes quiet — or at least quieter. Deliberately spend time in them, not as a vacation from the problem but as deliberate retraining data. The nervous system learns by experience. If the experience base is mostly high-threat, the calibration stays high. Adding low-threat experience data, consistently, over time, gives the system new training data. You are not overwriting the old program — you are adding new inputs that give the system permission to update its threat model. This takes time. It is not linear. It works.

The System Is Not Your Enemy

The hypervigilant system kept you alive in the environment that built it. That is not a small thing. The scan, the threat-detection sensitivity, the ability to read rooms and read people and anticipate danger before it arrives — those capabilities came at a cost, and they also produced real operational value in the contexts that required them.

The goal is not to destroy the system. It's to give it accurate data about the current environment so it can calibrate appropriately. A threat-detection system with an accurate threat map is an asset. The same system running a 2006 threat map in 2026 is a liability. The map is the problem, not the system.

You can't just relax your way out of this. But you can run a recalibration protocol — deliberately, consistently, over time — that gives your nervous system the evidence it needs to lower the sensitivity setting. The system will follow the data. It always has. Give it better data.
