Cognitive Science for Operators

Cognitive Distortions: 10 Ways Your Brain Lies to You (And How to Catch It)

By Ryan Bardyla · Canadian Army Veteran · From Stubborn Bastard

The Brain Is Not an Accuracy Machine

Your brain processes somewhere in the range of 70,000 thoughts per day. A significant portion of them are wrong — not wrong in the sense of "a different perspective could be valid" but wrong in the sense of logically invalid, factually inaccurate, or systematically distorted by predictable error patterns that have been documented, named, and studied for fifty years.

The brain's primary job is not accuracy. Its primary job is prediction and threat-avoidance. It will sacrifice truth for speed every time, because speed was what kept you alive in the environment that built this hardware. A brain that takes thirty seconds to carefully evaluate whether the rustling in the grass is a predator produces fewer ancestors than a brain that fires the threat response in 200 milliseconds and asks questions later.

The distortions below are not character defects. They are predictable artifacts of a prediction system optimized for speed over accuracy. Knowing their names doesn't make you immune to them. But it gives you a diagnostic vocabulary. You cannot challenge a distortion you cannot identify. Name it first. Challenge it second.

"Your brain doesn't generate thoughts. It generates predictions. The distortions are prediction errors — the same kinds that corrupted every intelligence system ever built. Knowing the error type is the first step to correcting the output."

The Ten Distortions

01
All-or-Nothing Thinking
Also called black-and-white thinking. The brain collapses a spectrum into two positions: perfect or worthless, success or failure, in or out. There is no middle ground, no partial credit, no gradations. A single imperfection invalidates the whole.
Example: "I missed one workout. The entire week is ruined." / "I made one mistake on the project. I'm terrible at this job."
02
Catastrophizing
The brain selects the worst possible outcome from the available range of possibilities and treats it as the most probable one. The jump from "this could go wrong" to "this will definitely go wrong in the worst way" happens in a single step, without a probability assessment.
Example: "My boss wants to see me. I'm getting fired." / "I have a headache. What if it's something serious?"
03
Mind Reading
The brain generates a confident assessment of what another person is thinking — typically something negative — without any direct evidence. The assessment feels like knowledge rather than inference. It is inference. The evidence base is usually a facial expression, a silence, or the absence of an expected response.
Example: "He didn't reply. He's angry at me." / "She seemed quiet at dinner. She's definitely upset about something I did."
04
Fortune Telling
The brain predicts a negative future outcome as if it were already determined. The prediction is not framed as a concern or a possibility — it is framed as a known fact about the future. This removes the possibility of different outcomes from the working model and collapses planning to "how to cope with the inevitable failure."
Example: "I know how this interview is going to go." / "There's no point trying — I already know it won't work out."
05
Emotional Reasoning
The brain uses emotional state as evidence of factual truth. "I feel it, therefore it is." Because the feeling is real and intense, the conclusion drawn from it must also be real. This is the logic that produces certainty from emotion — and it is one of the most reliable generators of false conclusions, because feelings are data about your emotional state, not about external reality.
Example: "I feel like a failure, therefore I am one." / "I feel guilty, so I must have done something wrong."
06
Should Statements
The brain applies rigid rules about how things must be — how you should behave, how others should act, how the world should operate. When reality fails to comply with the rules, the response is guilt (self-directed) or frustration and resentment (other-directed). The rules themselves are rarely examined for accuracy or usefulness.
Example: "I should have handled that better." / "He should know how I feel without me having to say it."
07
Personalization
The brain assigns excessive personal responsibility for events outside your control. You are the cause of outcomes that involved multiple factors, other people's choices, and circumstances you could not determine. This can run in both directions — self-blame for bad outcomes, and overclaiming credit for others' responses.
Example: "The team missed the deadline because of me." / "She's in a bad mood — I must have done something."
08
Overgeneralization
A single negative event becomes a permanent, universal pattern. One instance produces a rule that applies everywhere, always. The words "always" and "never" are signature markers. A conclusion that requires a sample size of one to establish a pattern that applies to all cases is not a conclusion — it's a first data point.
Example: "I always mess things up right when they matter." / "Things never work out for me."
09
Mental Filter
The brain filters out positive data and focuses exclusively on one negative detail, using it to color the entire picture. The negative detail is real; the filtering of everything else is the distortion. An entire performance review with fifteen strong points and one area of development is processed as: "I got negative feedback."
Example: "Someone at the presentation looked bored the whole time." (Ignoring 30 engaged faces.) / "The date went well except for that one awkward moment."
10
Labeling
Instead of describing a specific behavior, the brain attaches a global identity label. A behavior becomes an identity. A single failure becomes a permanent classification. Labels are compressed — they carry enormous weight in very few words, and they are nearly impossible to argue against because they have been made unfalsifiable by their global scope.
Example: "I'm a fraud." / "I'm a failure." / "I'm the kind of person who can't commit."

The Challenge Protocol

TOOL 01
Name the Distortion First
Before doing anything else, identify which distortion is operating. Not all at once — find the primary one. The naming itself creates distance. "I am worthless" is identity. "I am having a Labeling distortion — the thought is that I am worthless" is observation. That gap is where the challenge protocol has room to operate. You cannot challenge what you're inside of. Name it to get outside of it.
TOOL 02
The Evidence Test
State the thought as a claim. Then run both sides: What is the actual evidence that this claim is true? What evidence exists that it is not? State both as observable facts, not as feelings (feelings go in the emotional reasoning distortion — they are not evidence of external truth). Most distorted thoughts produce significantly weaker cases than they seemed when running unchallenged. The brain does not check its own work. You have to run the audit manually.
TOOL 03
The Alternative Explanation
What else could explain this? The first explanation the brain produces is the threat-detection draft — fast, high-confidence, and built for speed, not accuracy. It is not the only possible explanation. It may not even be the most probable one. Generate at least two alternative explanations for the same set of facts. Not to pick the most comforting — to establish that the brain's first read was not the only read. Multiple competing explanations immediately reduce the distorted thought's authority.
TOOL 04
The Probability Calibration
Specifically for catastrophizing and fortune-telling: rate the probability of the worst-case outcome on a 0–100 scale. Then rate the probability of the most realistic outcome. Then rate the probability of the best-case outcome. Write all three. The brain defaults to presenting the catastrophe as the most likely scenario — the explicit probability ratings often reveal that the catastrophic outcome is assigned 90% probability while, under honest examination, 15% is more defensible. The rating makes the distortion visible as a distortion.

This Is Not a Cure

Naming cognitive distortions does not make them stop. The brain will continue generating them — they are features of the threat-prediction system, not bugs you can patch out. What changes with this vocabulary is your relationship to the output.

Right now, the thoughts arrive and they feel like reports. Like intelligence briefings from a source you've been trained to trust. The distortion framework gives you a tool to assess the source's reliability: this briefing was authored by Catastrophizing, and Catastrophizing has a documented pattern of overstating threat probability. That doesn't mean the threat isn't real. It means you need to run secondary verification before acting on it.

You cannot trust a system you don't understand. You've been working from the brain's first draft your entire life. This is the beginning of editing it.
