A Decision Framework

Should I Quit? The Protocol Nobody Gave You Permission to Use

By Ryan Bardyla · Canadian Army Veteran · From Stubborn Bastard

The Lie They Sold You

Every self-help book says the same thing: never quit. Warriors don't quit. Grind through the pain. Your breakthrough is one more rep away.

They're wrong. And the reason they're wrong is survivorship bias — we only hear from the people who persisted and succeeded. Nobody writes a book called I Kept Going and It Destroyed Me, even though that book would outsell the others three to one.

The "warrior who never quits" narrative is seductive because it's clean. It asks nothing complicated of you. Just keep your head down and push. The problem is that it makes no distinction between the hardness that builds you and the hardness that breaks you. It treats all suffering as productive, all persistence as virtue. That's not wisdom. That's a liability.

The guy grinding himself into powder at a job that's consuming his health, his family, and his self-respect isn't being tough. He's being stupid. And he's calling it strength so he doesn't have to face the harder question.

There are three kinds of tenacity, and only one of them is worth a damn.

Stupid Tenacity is persisting because you're too proud to admit the thing isn't working. It sounds like: "I've already invested ten years" — the sunk cost fallacy in a tactical vest. Or: "What will people think if I walk away?" — performance over reality. Or the worst one: "Warriors don't give up" — using identity as a reason to ignore evidence.

Survival Tenacity is persisting because the alternative is actual collapse — financial ruin, your kids going hungry, a cliff with no bottom. This one is real, and it deserves respect. Sometimes you stay because there is no viable exit right now. That's not weakness. That's operational reality.

Strategic Persistence is staying because you have clear evidence that continuing serves the outcome you've deliberately chosen. It's not stubbornness. It's decision-making under pressure.

Most people running on Stupid Tenacity believe they're operating in Strategic Persistence. The protocol is how you tell the difference.

The Real Question

When someone asks "should I quit?" they're usually asking the wrong question. The question isn't whether something is hard. Of course it's hard. Everything worth doing is hard. The question is whether the hardness is building you or consuming you.

There is hard that builds. It produces skill, resilience, competence, and identity. The difficulty is the mechanism of development. You can feel it — not comfortable, but directional. Like a muscle that's fatigued from use rather than injured from misuse.

Then there is hard that breaks. It produces nothing except damage — to your body, your relationships, your sense of self. It doesn't make you stronger. It makes you smaller. And the worst part is that both kinds of hard feel similar in the moment. The body responds to both with the same stress chemistry. The mind tends to apply the same narrative: this is supposed to be hard, keep going.

Stupid Tenacity sounds like: "I've already put in two years — I can't walk away now." Sunk costs are irrelevant to whether continuing is smart. What you've already spent is gone regardless of what you do next. The only question is what the next year produces.

It sounds like: "What will people think if I quit?" This one is just performance. You're spending your life currency maintaining someone else's perception of you. That's an expensive way to live.

Strategic Persistence sounds different: "Is this aligned with who I'm actually trying to become?" "What's the honest cost of staying another year?" "What does the evidence say — not what do I hope, but what does the data say?"

"The courage to quit is rarer than the courage to persist. Staying is passive. You don't have to decide anything. Leaving requires you to face judgment, uncertainty, and the void."

The Quit/Stay Protocol

Four questions. Answer them honestly. The protocol doesn't make the decision for you — it makes the decision visible.

The Quit/Stay Protocol — Four Questions
1
Is this building me or breaking me?
Not "is it hard?" — everything is hard. Is the hardness producing something? Competence, clarity, identity? Or is it producing damage? Rate the last 90 days honestly.
2
Would I start this today, knowing what I know now?
Strip out the sunk cost. Forget the years invested. If you were handed this situation fresh tomorrow — same terms, same conditions, same trajectory — would you sign on? If the answer is no, you already have your answer.
3
What is the actual cost of staying one more year?
Not the financial cost. The full cost. Your health, your relationships, your self-respect, your time. Write the number. Most people discover they've never actually computed it.
4
Who benefits from my persistence?
If the honest answer is "not me," that's important information. Sometimes staying serves others at your expense. That might be necessary. But it should be a conscious choice, not a default.

The Courage Reframe

Here's what nobody tells you: staying is passive. You don't have to decide anything. The situation continues by default. Leaving requires an active decision. You have to face the judgment, the uncertainty, and the fog of not knowing what comes next.

Walking away from something that's consuming you is not defeat. It is strategic withdrawal. It is choosing your ground. Every competent military operator knows that the most dangerous thing you can do is stay in a compromised position because you don't want to look like you're retreating. You hold ground when it's worth holding. You fall back when the position is untenable. That's not cowardice. That's tactics.

The "never quit" crowd confuses enduring with winning. They're not the same thing. You can endure indefinitely at a cost you can't afford. Strategic persistence means choosing which hills are worth dying on — and accepting that most of them aren't.

"I'd rather be a quitter with my dignity intact than a success who had to kill his soul to get there. The second one isn't actually success. It's just a very expensive failure with better optics."

The permission to walk away from something that isn't working is not given to you by anyone. You have to give it to yourself. The protocol is the tool. The decision is yours.

If you're asking "should I quit?" — you've been carrying that question long enough that it's worth running the protocol. Not because the protocol tells you what to do. Because it makes visible what you already know.
